### Nature and Scope of Comparative Politics: From Aristotle to the Modern Era
- **Archival Reference:** `page_001.png` to `page_003.png` (Evolution & Definition).
- **Aristotle as the Father of Comparative Politics:**
  - Comparative politics is as ancient as political philosophy itself. Aristotle was profoundly influenced by his father Nicomachus, who was a court physician, leading Aristotle to apply the empirical classification methods of biology to politics.
  - Aristotle systematically collected, analyzed, and compared **158 Greek city-state constitutions**, classifying regimes based on two empirical criteria: (1) the number of rulers (one, few, many), and (2) the ethical purpose of rule (common interest vs selfish interest).
  - His six-fold classification of constitutions remains the first foundational empirical study in comparative methodology.
- **Definition of Comparative Politics:** The systematic empirical analysis and comparison of domestic political systems, state structures, processes, political behavior, and institutions across different sovereign states.
- **Two Major Historical Phases of Evolution:**
  1. *Traditional Comparative Politics:* From classical Greece until the Second World War.
  2. *Modern Comparative Politics:* Post-WWII, driven by decolonization and the Behavioral Revolution.

### Limitations of Traditional Comparative Politics
- **Archival Reference:** `page_003.png` to `page_005.png` (Critique of Traditional Approach).
- **Roy Macridis's Indictment (*The Study of Comparative Government*, 1955):** Macridis and contemporary behavioral political scientists identified four fatal analytical flaws in traditional comparative politics:
  1. *Parochial and Eurocentric:* Focused almost exclusively on a handful of Western European democracies (Britain, France, Germany) and the United States, completely ignoring Asian, African, and Latin American political communities.
  2. *Descriptive rather than Analytical:* Replaced rigorous theoretical hypothesis-testing with tedious descriptive catalogues of legal statutes, constitutional clauses, and historical narratives.
  3. *Formalistic and Legalistic:* Studied formal, de jure government organs (Parliament, Executive, Judiciary, Written Constitutions) while completely neglecting informal, dynamic political forces (caste, religion, tribes, interest groups, extra-constitutional power networks).
  4. *Static and Non-Comparative:* Merely juxtaposed parallel country chapters side-by-side without developing universal analytical categories or comparative conceptual frameworks.

### The Behavioral Revolution and the Emergence of Modern Comparative Politics
- **Archival Reference:** `page_005.png` to `page_008.png` (Post-WWII Behavioral Turn).
- **Catalysts of the Post-WWII Paradigm Shift:**
  1. *Decolonization:* The emergence of dozens of newly independent nation-states in Asia, Africa, and the Caribbean. Western constitutional blueprints proved utterly incapable of explaining military coups, tribal patronage, and one-party states in the post-colonial world.
  2. *The Behavioral Revolution:* The intellectual movement in American social sciences (University of Chicago, SSRC Committee on Comparative Politics) demanding scientific rigor, quantification, empirical field research, and interdisciplinary integration with sociology, psychology, and anthropology.
- **From "Government" to "Political System":** The term "Government" was abandoned because it denoted only formal state organs. It was replaced by **"Political System"**, which encompasses both formal institutions and all informal societal structures that perform political functions.

### David Easton's Systems Approach: The Input-Output Model
- **Archival Reference:** `page_008.png` to `page_010.png` (The Political System, 1953).
- **Core Definition:** David Easton defined the political system as the set of interactions through which values are authoritatively allocated for a society: **"Authoritative Allocation of Values"**.
- **The Systems Architecture (The Black Box Model):**
  1. *The Environment:* The broader social, economic, cultural, and ecological setting within which the political system operates.
  2. *Inputs:* Flowing from the environment into the political system, consisting of:
     - **Demands:** Claims made by individuals and groups for action (demands for higher wages, better roads, welfare, civil rights).
     - **Supports:** The willingness of citizens to accept the decisions of the system, pay taxes, obey laws, and show civic loyalty.
  3. *The Black Box (The Conversion Mechanism):* The internal governmental processes (legislatures, bureaucracies, executives) that process raw inputs and convert them into binding policies.
  4. *Outputs:* Authoritative decisions, policies, laws, taxation, and resource distributions enacted by the system.
  5. *Feedback Loop:* The mechanism through which outputs affect the environment, generating new demands and supports, allowing the political system to adapt and maintain equilibrium.

### Gabriel Almond's Structural-Functional Approach
- **Archival Reference:** `page_010.png` to `page_012.png` (The Politics of the Developing Areas, 1960).
- **Why Structural-Functionalism?** Easton's model was criticized for treating the internal conversion mechanism as an impenetrable "black box." Gabriel Almond opened the black box by analyzing specific structures and the universal functions they perform.
- **Universal Axiom:** All political systems have structures that perform universal political functions; structures may differ radically between advanced Western democracies and developing societies, but the basic functions are universal.
- **Almond's Two Functional Dimensions:**
  - *Input Functions (Performed primarily by non-governmental societal structures):*
    1. *Political Socialization and Recruitment:* Transmitting political values across generations and recruiting citizens into political roles (Family, Schools, Media).
    2. *Interest Articulation:* Expressing sectoral demands and grievances into the political arena (Pressure Groups, Trade Unions, Tribal Associations).
    3. *Interest Aggregation:* Combining diverse sectoral demands into coherent policy platforms (Political Parties).
    4. *Political Communication:* Transmitting political information throughout the system (Free Press, Mass Media, Social Networks).
  - *Output Functions (Performed primarily by governmental structures):*
    1. *Rule-Making:* Drafting binding legislation (Legislature).
    2. *Rule-Application:* Implementing and administering laws (Executive & Bureaucracy).
    3. *Rule-Adjudication:* Interpreting laws and resolving disputes (Judiciary).
