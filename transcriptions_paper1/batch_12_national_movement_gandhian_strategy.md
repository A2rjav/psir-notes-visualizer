## 1. THE EVOLUTIONARY TRAJECTORY OF THE NATIONAL MOVEMENT: MODERATES TO EXTREMISTS

### A. Bipin Chandra's Re-interpretation of the Moderates
Traditional historiography (especially Imperialist and early Marxist schools) dismissed the Moderates (1885–1905) as elite armchair politicians practicing the "politics of mendicancy" through petitions, prayers, and protests. However, Marxist-Nationalist historian **Bipin Chandra** (*India's Struggle for Independence*) offers a profound structural revision:
1. **Ideological Counter-Hegemony:** While Moderates lacked a mass base and could not launch physical mass struggles, they waged struggle at the **ideological level**.
2. **Economic Critique of Colonialism:** Thinkers like Dadabhai Naoroji (*Poverty and Un-British Rule in India*), Romesh Chunder Dutt (*Economic History of India*), and Mahadev Govind Ranade formulated the seminal **"Drain of Wealth" theory**. By exposing that British rule was inherently exploitative and systematically de-industrializing India, they shattered the British myth of the "civilizing mission" (*benevolent despotism*).
3. **Target Audience of Memorials:** As **Justice M. G. Ranade** famously observed:
   > *"The memorials and petitions are ostensibly addressed to the British government, but in reality, they are addressed to our own people."*
   Their primary historical function was political education and awakening a pan-Indian national consciousness.
4. **Gokhale's Philosophy of Historical Sacrifice:** Gopal Krishna Gokhale recognized the transitional constraints of their historical moment:
   > *"We are at such a stage when our achievements are bound to be small and our failures too frequent. We must content ourselves by serving our country by our failures. It is through these failures that the strength will grow."*

### B. The Extremist Phase: Militant Nationalism and Direct Action (1905–1919)
The failure of the Moderates to extract meaningful constitutional concessions, combined with Lord Curzon's reactionary and imperialist policies (Calcutta Corporation Act 1899, Indian Universities Act 1904, and the 1905 Partition of Bengal), triggered the rise of **Militant Nationalism** led by the **Lal-Bal-Pal triumvirate** (Lala Lajpat Rai, Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal) and **Sri Aurobindo Ghosh**.

| Feature | Moderates (1885–1905) | Extremists (1905–1919) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Social Base** | Urban, English-educated elites, upper-middle-class professionals (lawyers, doctors, journalists). | Lower-middle classes, students, urban youth, vernacular intelligentsia. |
| **Ideological Premise** | Believed in the providential nature of British connection; believed British could be reasoned with via British justice. | Believed British rule was irreconcilably antagonistic to Indian growth; drew inspiration from indigenous cultural symbols. |
| **Political Goal** | Self-government within the British Empire (Dominion Status) achieved through gradual legislative reform. | Swaraj as a Birthright (Tilak); Purna Swaraj / Complete Independence (Aurobindo Ghosh was the earliest advocate). |
| **Methods & Tactics** | Constitutional agitation, 3P's (Petition, Prayer, Protest), legislative debates, press memorials. | Direct Action, Swadeshi, Boycott of British goods, National Education, Passive Resistance, Swadeshi Melas. |
| **Concept of Resistance**| Loyalty to the legal framework; reform from within. | **"Praja Droh"** (Right of the ruled to rebel/resist an unjust, exploitative ruler); Tilak: *"Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it."* |
| **Historical Contribution**| Created the all-India ideological framework and national identity. | Infused courage, self-reliance (*Atmashakti*), mass enthusiasm, and radicalized Indian politics, laying the launchpad for Gandhi. |

### A. Typology of Revolutions and Mass Movements (Bipin Chandra's Thesis)
According to Bipin Chandra, the Indian National Movement occupies a historically singular place among modern political revolutions:
1. **Scale and Longevity:** It was the largest mass movement in human history that sustained itself over three decades and reached its logical culmination in national liberation without degenerating into militarized chaos.
2. **Controlled Character:** Unlike the French Revolution (1789), which was an uncontrolled, spontaneous, and violent eruption that succumbed to the Reign of Terror and Bonapartism, the Gandhian movement was an intensely **controlled mass mobilization**. The leadership retained operational command over when to escalate, when to de-escalate, and when to pause.
3. **Protracted Nature:** While the Russian Bolshevik Revolution of October 1917 or the Chinese Communist Revolution relied on rapid/overnight insurrectionary seizures of state power in the face of collapsing regimes, the Indian struggle against the British Raj was a **protracted war of exhaustion and counter-hegemony**.

### B. The Gramscian Lens: War of Position vs. War of Maneuver
Antonio Gramsci in his *Prison Notebooks* drew a critical distinction between two revolutionary strategies based on the nature of the state being challenged:
- **War of Maneuver (Movement):** Frontal assault and rapid military/insurrectionary overthrow of the state machinery. Gramsci argued this works only in "the East" (e.g., Tsarist Russia 1917), where the state is everything (*autocratic, brittle*) and civil society is "primordial and gelatinous."
- **War of Position:** A protracted, cultural, and political struggle waged over trenches and earthworks of **civil society** to erode the ruling class's moral and intellectual leadership (*hegemony*) and build an alternative counter-hegemony. This is essential in "the West", where the state is backed by a resilient, dense civil society.

#### Application to the Indian National Movement:
1. **The Nature of the Colonial State:** The British Raj in India was not a purely naked, despotic terror-state (like Tsarist Russia or fascist regimes). It operated as a **"semi-hegemonic" and "semi-democratic" constitutional autocracy**.
   - It relied on the manufactured belief among Indians in the British **"Rule of Law"**, fairness, efficient administration, police protection, social reforms, and modern education.
   - Many Indians genuinely believed that British rule was superior to the arbitrary rule of native princes.
2. **Gandhi's Realization:** As Bipin Chandra highlights, Gandhi understood that an armed insurrection (War of Maneuver) against a modernized imperialist military state would invite swift and devastating state violence, crushing the unorganized masses.
3. **Two-Level Combat:**
   - **War of Position (Primary & Continuous):** Gandhi conducted a decades-long struggle to de-mystify and de-legitimize the British Raj, demonstrating to the Indian masses that colonial rule was root-and-branch exploitative, parasitic, and morally bankrupt. Simultaneously, through the **Constructive Programme**, he built counter-hegemonic institutions (Panchayats, national schools, charkha, khadi, ashrams).
   - **War of Maneuver (Episodic & Tactical):** Direct, active confrontations (Non-Cooperation, Civil Disobedience, Quit India) where the masses stepped out to openly defy state authority, paralyze administration, and court arrest.

## 3. STRUGGLE-TRUCE-STRUGGLE (S-T-S) STRATEGY VS. STRUGGLE-VICTORY (S-V)

```
===================================================================================================
                GANDHIAN STRUGGLE - TRUCE - STRUGGLE (S-T-S) CYCLE
===================================================================================================

           STRUGGLE PHASE (Active)
           • Mass agitation, boycotts, law-breaking
           • High emotional enthusiasm & sacrifice
           • Confrontation with state machinery
                     │
                     │  [Called off before mass exhaustion & brutal state crackdown]
                     ▼
             TRUCE PHASE (Passive / Latent)
           • Formal negotiation or tactical retreat
           • Constructive Programme: Khadi, Untouchability, Communal Unity
           • Moral replenishment, psychological healing, organizational expansion
                     │
                     │  [Next offensive launched at higher ideological plateau]
                     ▼
           STRUGGLE PHASE (Escalated Active)
           • CDM (1930) > NCM (1920) > Quit India (1942)
           • Increasing mass involvement and political maturity
                     │
                     ▼
              VICTORY (Swaraj & De-colonization)
```

### A. The S-V vs S-T-S Paradigm
- **Struggle-Victory (S-V) Strategy:** The classical Marxist-Leninist or militarist model where an agitation, once launched, must continuously escalate until the state is violently overthrown. Pausing or retreating is viewed as compromise or bourgeois betrayal.
- **Struggle-Truce-Struggle (S-T-S) Strategy:** Bipin Chandra demonstrates that Gandhi invented the **S-T-S strategy** as the only viable mechanism for a democratic, non-violent mass movement fighting a semi-constitutional colonial state.

### B. Why Was S-T-S Strategically Necessary?
1. **The Psychology of the Masses:**
   - Gandhi was a master mass psychologist. He knew that the **capacity of ordinary human beings to endure suffering, sacrifice livelihoods, withstand police lathis, and stay in jail is inherently limited**.
   - No mass movement can remain at a fever pitch indefinitely. If a movement is dragged out beyond its psychological endurance, enthusiasm wanes, frustration sets in, apathy takes over, and the movement collapses ignominiously from within.
2. **Gandhi's Golden Tactical Rule:**
   - *Gandhi called off movements when they were at their apparent peak, before the signs of exhaustion, fracture, or violence appeared on the surface.*
3. **Master Strategist — Dictating the Terms:**
   - Gandhi **never left the initiative or the ball in the court of the adversary**.
   - The **time, place, and issue of confrontation were always Gandhi's choice** (e.g., choosing Salt in 1930 baffled both the Viceroy Lord Irwin and the Congress Working Committee).
   - His strategy was fundamentally **proactive, never merely reactive**.
4. **The Critical Function of the Truce Phase:**
   - The "Truce" phase was never an idle surrender. It was an active phase of **Constructive Work** (*Rachnatmak Karyakram*):
     - Eradication of Untouchability (*Harijan Sevak Sangh*).
     - Promotion of Khadi and Village Industries (economic self-reliance).
     - Fostering Hindu-Muslim fraternity.
     - Spreading Basic Education (*Nai Talim*).
     - Deepening the organizational roots of Congress into rural and mohalla committees.
   - This ensured that when the next Struggle phase erupted, the movement began from a **higher qualitative and organizational plateau**.

### A. Causes of the Movement
1. **Rowlatt Act (1919):** Prolongation of wartime emergency restrictions; detention without trial; suppression of civil liberties.
2. **Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (13 April 1919):** Ruthless slaughter of unarmed citizens; martial law in Punjab; public floggings; British House of Lords eulogizing Dyer and raising a fund of £26,000 for him deeply wounded Indian national honor.
3. **Hunter Committee Report:** Whitewashed the imperial atrocities, escalating popular disillusionment.
4. **Post-World War I Economic Distress:** Crushing inflation, wartime taxes, price rise of essential commodities, famine, and the influenza pandemic.
5. **The Khilafat Question:** Dismemberment of the Ottoman Empire and removal of the Caliph's temporal powers alienated Indian Muslims. Gandhi astutely identified this as an unprecedented opportunity to forge an anti-imperialist **Hindu-Muslim alliance** (*"an opportunity which will not recur for another hundred years"*).
6. **Psychological Readiness:** Masses were seething with indignation and actively seeking a new, militant avenue of action beyond petitions.

### B. Strategy and Programme of Non-Cooperation
- **Nagpur Session of Congress (December 1920):**
  - Officially adopted the Gandhian programme of **extra-constitutional mass struggle**.
  - Redefined the goal of Congress: *"Attainment of Swaraj through peaceful and legitimate means"* (replacing the old Moderate creed of constitutional means).
  - Reorganized the Congress structure down to village/mohalla committees, transforming it from an elite conclave into a mass political machine.
- **Gandhi's Conceptual Definition:**
  > *"Non-cooperation with evil is as much a duty as is cooperation with good. It is the inherent right of the citizens to refuse cooperation with a ruler who misrules."*
- **Slogan & Goal:** Attainment of **"Swaraj within a year"** (a motivational catalyst designed to galvanize mass imagination).

### C. The Chauri Chaura Withdrawal (February 1922) and Historiographical Controversy
On 5 February 1922, an agitated crowd at Chauri Chaura (Gorakhpur, UP) was fired upon by the police. The mob retaliated by burning down the police station, killing 22 policemen. Gandhi unilaterally withdrew the nationwide movement on 12 February 1922 (Bardoli Resolution).

#### 1. Contemporary and Marxist Critique:
- **Jawaharlal Nehru & Subhas Chandra Bose:** Expressed utter bewilderment. Bose called it a *"national calamity"*, asking why the whole nation should be penalized for the stray misconduct of an isolated village in Gorakhpur.
- **M. N. Roy & R. Palme Dutt (*India Today*):** Formulated the classic **Marxist Critique**:
  - Argued that Gandhi was fundamentally a **bourgeois leader fearful of the radicalization of the masses**.
  - Claimed that when the movement began turning into an agrarian anti-landlord, anti-tax struggle (threatening the property rights of Indian zamindars and capitalists), Gandhi manufactured Chauri Chaura as a pretext to abandon the revolution.

#### 2. Bipin Chandra's Re-evaluation (The Nationalist-Strategic Defence):
Bipin Chandra decisively refutes the Marxist conspiracy thesis:
1. **Fraying National Unity:** The non-violent fabric and inter-communal solidarity were visibly fracturing by late 1921 (e.g., the communalization and violence of the **Mappila / Moplah Rebellion** in Malabar).
2. **Mass Exhaustion:** After nearly two years of sustained confrontation, the urban middle class and students were drifting back to schools and courts; the masses were becoming fatigued.
3. **Preempting Disproportionate State Repression:**
   - Once a movement turns violent, a modern state's moral inhibitions vanish.
   - The British government was waiting for a major pretext of violence to deploy massive military firepower and unleash brutal, unrestrained state terror.
   - Had the British crushed the movement under the wheel of martial law, the Indian masses would have been traumatized, demoralized, and paralyzed for decades.
4. **Graceful Exit:** Gandhi deliberately shouldered all public opprobrium, protected the unorganized masses from imperial vengeance, and executed a **masterful strategic retreat** to preserve nationalist cadre and organizational capital for the next wave.

### D. Assessment: Failure or Triumph?
- **Formal Failure:** The explicit target of *"Swaraj in a year"* was not realized.
- **Substantive / Structural Triumph:**
  1. **Debunked the "Microscopic Minority" Myth:** Proved Lord Dufferin's thesis wrong by mobilizing millions across provinces, communities, and castes.
  2. **Multi-Class Character:** Peasants, workers, artisans, shopkeepers, professionals, and women entered the political arena for the first time.
  3. **Demystification of Fear:** Transformed passive subjects into fearless political actors; going to colonial prison became a badge of honor rather than a social disgrace.

### A. Conceptual Roots: Henry David Thoreau's Philosophy
While Non-Cooperation was largely rooted in Tolstoy's philosophy of non-participation with evil, Civil Disobedience drew direct inspiration from **Henry David Thoreau's 1849 essay *Resistance to Civil Government (Civil Disobedience)***:
- Thoreau asserted that when a government enacts unjust laws, the only place for a just and moral citizen is prison.
- Gandhi adopted Thoreau's formulation: **Civil disobedience is the peaceful, public, conscientiously deliberate breach of an immoral or unjust statutory law enacted by an illegitimate authority**.
- Unlike a common criminal who breaks the law covertly for private gain, a civil resister violates the law **openly, peacefully, and willingly accepts the state's punishment** to shame the moral conscience of the ruler.

### B. Historical Catalyst & Pre-conditions (1928–1930)
After seven years of the Truce/Constructive phase, the objective conditions for a new struggle ripened:
1. **The Simon Commission (1927):** An all-white commission sent to decide India's constitutional destiny; greeted with *"Simon Go Back"* black flag protests.
2. **Martyrdom of Lala Lajpat Rai (1928):** Brutally lathi-charged during Simon protests, electrifying national anti-British sentiment.
3. **The Great Depression (1929):** Agricultural commodity prices crashed by over 50%, devastating Indian peasantry who were crushed under rigid land revenue and debt burdens.
4. **Lahore Congress (1929):** Under Jawaharlal Nehru's presidency, Congress hoisted the tricolor and declared **Purna Swaraj** (Complete Independence) as its irreducible goal, nominating 26 January 1930 as Independence Day.

### D. The Genius of the Salt Satyagraha & Dandi March
When Lord Irwin ignored the 11 points, Gandhi launched the **Dandi March** (12 March – 6 April 1930), walking 240 miles from Sabarmati Ashram to Dandi to break the Salt Law.
1. **Symbolic Universalism:** Salt was consumed by every human being—Hindu, Muslim, upper-caste, Dalit, rich, and poor. The British tax on salt was the most graphic illustration of colonial callousness toward human survival.
2. **Rural & Women's Mobilization:** Breaking the salt law could be practiced along India's vast coastline by every ordinary peasant, woman, and child. It sparked an unprecedented mass awakening where women emerged in thousands to picket foreign cloth and liquor shops.

# PART 3: SCHOLARLY PERSPECTIVES, QUOTES & EXAM-READY VALUE ADDITIONS

### 1. Key Scholars & Conceptual Formulations
* **Bipin Chandra (*India's Struggle for Independence*):**
  - Characterizes Gandhian strategy as a **protracted War of Position** operating on the **Struggle-Truce-Struggle (S-T-S)** dialectic.
  - Highlights that the Indian National Movement was the only modern mass movement that fought against a semi-democratic/constitutional colonial state and dismantled its moral authority step by step.
* **Antonio Gramsci (*Selections from the Prison Notebooks*):**
  - Provides the theoretical categories of **Hegemony**, **War of Position**, and **War of Maneuver**. Colonial rule was sustained through hegemony (consent via "Rule of Law"), which required counter-hegemony before state power could be dislodged.
* **Judith Brown (*Gandhi's Rise to Power: Indian Politics 1915–1922*):**
  - Advanced the **"Sub-contractor thesis"**: Gandhi mobilized India by recruiting influential local leaders (*sub-contractors*—local notables, rich peasants, lawyers, caste leaders) who in turn mobilized the masses.
* **Sumit Sarkar (*Modern India 1885–1947*):**
  - Analyzes the multi-class dynamics and tensions within the Gandhian movement. Points out the constant dialectic between **"Grammarian" control from above** (Gandhian leadership) and **spontaneous radical pressures from below** (peasant no-tax struggles, tribal forest satyagrahas, working-class strikes).
* **Ranajit Guha (*Elementary Aspects of Peasant Insurgency in Colonial India*):**
  - Represents the **Subaltern Studies School**. Argues that elite nationalist historiography overlooked the autonomous domain of subaltern politics, where peasants and tribals interpreted Gandhi in their own radical, millenarian terms (*"Gandhi Raj"*).
