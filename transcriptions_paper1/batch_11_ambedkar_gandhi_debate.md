# SECTION II: THE GANDHI-AMBEDKAR DEBATE: AN EXHAUSTIVE SCHOLARLY ANALYSIS

The historic polemic between Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (1869–1948) and Dr. Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar (1891–1956) constitutes the single most important ideological and moral debate within 20th-century Indian political thought. As modern political theorists and historians—from Eleanor Zelliot and Christophe Jaffrelot to Arundhati Roy and Bhikhu Parekh—have established, this encounter was not merely a personal clash between two nationalist giants, but a deep confrontation between two irreconcilable ontologies of human emancipation, social justice, and national liberation.

### A. Philosophical Foundations: Moral Sanatan Reformism vs Structural Radical Annihilation

#### 1. Gandhi's Sanatan Framework
Gandhi approached caste as an insider, self-identifying as an orthodox *Sanatani Hindu*. His worldview was grounded in moral organicism, derived from the *Bhagavad Gita*, Leo Tolstoy, and John Ruskin:
- **Caste as Degeneration, Varna as Ideal**: Gandhi drew a sharp ontological distinction between *Varna* (the pristine cosmic order of functional specialization without hierarchical superiority) and *Caste* (*Jati*, which he condemned as an excrescence and a cancer that distorted the pure Hindu faith).
- **Untouchability as Sin**: For Gandhi, untouchability was the greatest plague on Hinduism—a moral blot (*Kalank*) that demanded spiritual atonement (*Prayaschitta*) by savarna (caste) Hindus. Emancipation was therefore conceived primarily as a process of moral repentance by the oppressor.

#### 2. Ambedkar's Radical Enlightenment Framework
Dr. Ambedkar approached caste with the rigorous sociological and juristic training of Columbia University (under John Dewey) and the London School of Economics:
- **Caste is Not an Accidental Aberration**: In *Annihilation of Caste* (1936), Ambedkar demonstrated that caste and untouchability are not mere degenerate deviations from a benign Varna ideal; they are the direct, logical, and inevitable outcome of the ideological superstructure of Brahmanical Hinduism.
- **Dynamic of Shastric Sanctity**: Unlike racial prejudice in the West, which violates Christian dogma, caste discrimination in India is sanctified and commanded by sacred Hindu texts (*Manusmriti*, *Vedas*, *Dharmashastras*). Therefore, appealing to the conscience of caste Hindus is futile because they are acting in faithful obedience to their religious scriptures.
- **The Call for Dynamiting the Scriptures**: Ambedkar famously declared:
  > *"You cannot build anything on the foundations of caste. You cannot build up a nation, you cannot build up a morality... You must give a new doctrinal basis to your life... You must destroy the authority of the Shastras and the Smritis."*

### B. Varna vs Caste: Textual Idealism vs Contextual Brutality

#### 1. The Division of Labour vs Division of Labourers
- **Gandhi's Defence**: Gandhi argued that Varna was humanity's most economical social arrangement, preventing cut-throat economic competition. By following the hereditary calling of one's ancestors, humanity conserves mental energy and avoids the capitalist rat-race.
- **Ambedkar's Devastating Refutation**: In Chapter 3 of *Annihilation of Caste*, Ambedkar provided an unassailable critique:
  1. *Unnatural Stratification*: The caste system is not merely a division of labour, but **a division of labourers into water-tight, graded compartments**.
  2. *Denial of Choice and Mobility*: It artificially pegs individual capacity to ancestral accident of birth rather than personal merit, aptitude, or inclination.
  3. *Economic Inefficiency & Degradation*: By stigmatizing manual and sanitary labour as polluting, it kills the dignity of labour and produces involuntary structural poverty.

#### 2. The Textual vs Contextual Fallacy
Shubhra Ranjan's notes emphasize Ambedkar's critique that **"Gandhi's view is textual (bookish), whereas caste is contextual"**:
- *Textual Varna*: An abstract, theoretical myth constructed in Brahminical texts where four varnas supposedly cooperate harmoniously.
- *Contextual Caste*: The lived reality of thousands of endogamous *Jatis* locked in a system of **Graded Inequality** (*Babasaheb's concept of Sopana-vyavastha*), where every caste except the highest Brahmin is both oppressor and oppressed, preventing horizontal solidarity.
- *Gandhi's Performative Contradiction*: Ambedkar pointed out that Gandhi himself was living proof of the breakdown of Varna: Gandhi, born into the Vaishya (Modh Bania) varna, was functioning as a political general (Kshatriya) and a moral preceptor/philosopher (Brahmin). Why should Dalits be chained to ancestral manual scavenging when Gandhi himself had transgressed his ancestral profession?

### C. Nomenclature & Agency: 'Harijan' Paternalism vs 'Dalit' Assertion


1. **Ambedkar's Critique of 'Harijan'**:
   - Ambedkar rejected the term *Harijan* as an insulting euphemism. If untouchables are "children of God", are the caste Hindus the children of monsters? Or does it imply that untouchables are illegitimate wards of upper-caste charity?
   - The term depoliticized the anti-caste movement, turning an urgent struggle for civil and political rights into a sentimental drama of upper-caste patronization.
2. **The Emergence of 'Dalit'**:
   - Ambedkar preferred the administrative term **"Depressed Classes"** for legal-constitutional representation, and philosophically pioneered the Marathi concept of **"Dalit"** (broken, oppressed), later popularized by the Dalit Panther movement in the 1970s.
   - "Dalit" carries revolutionary agency: it identifies oppression not as an immutable spiritual fate, but as a historical, human-made injustice to be smashed through democratic struggle.

1. **The Institutional Exclusion**:
   - Following the Poona Pact of 1932, Gandhi founded the *All India Anti-Untouchability League*, later renamed the *Harijan Sevak Sangh*.
   - Ambedkar was initially invited to draft a program. Ambedkar insisted that Dalits must hold executive and managerial positions in the organization, arguing that genuine emancipation is impossible without subaltern self-determination.
   - Gandhi flatly refused, stating that the Sangh was an instrument for **caste-Hindu penance**. Since it was the upper castes who had sinned, it was their exclusive duty to serve Dalits; Dalits should be passive recipients of this upper-caste benevolence.
2. **The 'Putana' Metaphor**:
   - In Hindu mythology (the *Bhagavata Purana*), King Kamsa sent the demoness Putana to kill the infant Krishna. Disguised as a beautiful, loving foster-mother, Putana offered her breast smeared with lethal poison.
   - Ambedkar argued that the Harijan Sevak Sangh performed the exact same function: under the sweet, loving guise of maternal welfare and upper-caste philanthropy, **it administered ideological poison**, neutralizing Dalit political militancy, keeping them tied to the Hindu fold, and preventing them from organizing independently for state power and legal rights.

### E. The Spatiality of Caste: Romanticised Gram Swaraj vs "Den of Localism and Ignorance"

The geographical and institutional arena of emancipation marked an unbridgeable gulf between Gandhi and Ambedkar:

1. **Gandhi's Romantic Pastoralism**:
   - In *Hind Swaraj* (1909), Gandhi envisioned independent India as an oceanic circle of self-governing village republics (*Gram Swaraj*). The village represented uncorrupted Indian civilizational virtue, free from the satanic vices of modern machinery, lawyers, railways, and centralized parliaments.
2. **Ambedkar's Constituent Assembly Indictment (November 4, 1948)**:
   - Introducing the Draft Constitution, Ambedkar fiercely rejected the inclusion of the village as the basic unit of the Indian polity:
     > *"What is a village but a sink of localism, a den of ignorance, narrow-mindedness and communalism? I am glad that the Draft Constitution has discarded the village and adopted the individual as the unit of society."*
   - To Ambedkar, the Indian village was not a republic of equals; it was a **republic of caste tyrants**. In every village, Dalits were physically segregated into sub-human settlements outside village boundaries, economically dependent on landlord castes, and subject to violent social boycotts if they demanded water rights or dignity.
   - **Urbanization as Liberation**: Ambedkar advised Dalits: *"Educate, Agitate, Organise"* and urged them to abandon the villages, migrate to industrial cities, acquire modern education, and look to the centralized constitutional state for protective laws.

#### 1. The Mahad Satyagraha (March 20, 1927) & Gandhi's Conspicuous Silence
- At the Chowdar Tank in Mahad (Maharashtra), Ambedkar led thousands of untouchables to drink water from a public reservoir legally opened to all citizens by the Bole Resolution. Caste Hindus attacked the peaceful Dalits and performed purification rituals (*Panchagavya*) on the tank using cow urine and dung.
- In December 1927, at the second Mahad conference, Ambedkar publicly burned the *Manusmriti*.
- **The Lecture Note Observation**: Shubhra Ranjan explicitly records:
  > *"Ambedkar was critical of Gandhi as Gandhi neither supported Mahad Satyagraha nor ever kept any fast for abolition of untouchability, though Gandhi has kept fast unto death to stop separate electorate for dalits."*
  Gandhi, who launched nationwide satyagrahas for peasant taxes (Kheda, Champaran) and salt, maintained an absolute tactical distance from Mahad, fearing it would divide the Hindu community and disrupt his multi-class national coalition.

#### 2. The Communal Award (August 1932) vs Yerwada Fast Unto Death
- At the Second Round Table Conference (1931) in London, Ambedkar brilliantly argued that Dalits were an independent political minority, separate from caste Hindus, needing constitutional protection just like Muslims and Sikhs.
- British Prime Minister Ramsay MacDonald announced the **Communal Award** (August 1932), granting the Depressed Classes **Separate Electorates**: Dalits would vote exclusively for Dalit candidates, plus vote in the general electorate (giving them dual voting power).
- **Gandhi's Fast Unto Death**: Imprisoned in Yerwada Jail (Pune), Gandhi declared a fast unto death on September 20, 1932. Gandhi argued that separate electorates would permanently vivisect the Hindu community, legally institutionalize untouchability, and disrupt village social life forever.
- **The Moral Blackmail**: Ambedkar regarded Gandhi's fast as a coercive political stunt. However, with Gandhi's life hanging by a thread and nationwide anti-Dalit pogroms threatened if Gandhi died, Ambedkar was cornered into a bitter compromise.
- **The Poona Pact (September 24, 1932)**:
  - Separate electorates were abandoned.
  - In return, Dalits were given **Reserved Seats** within the joint Hindu electorate (increased from 71 to 148 seats in provincial legislatures, and 18% in the Central Assembly).
  - *Ambedkar's Long-Term Verdict*: In *What Congress and Gandhi Have Done to the Untouchables* (1945), Ambedkar declared the Poona Pact an unmitigated disaster: reserved seats in a joint electorate ensured that only those Dalit candidates who acted as docile stooges (*chamchas*) of the dominant upper-caste majority could ever get elected.

```
│   and civil disobedience.               │   in independent India as "Grammar of    │

│   moral trustees of social wealth.      │   land, basic industries, key finance.   │
```

1. **Shift from Satyagraha to Constitutionalism**:
   - As noted in Shubhra Ranjan's notes (`page_066.png`): *"Initially Ambedkar adopted the method of satyagraha but later on shifted towards constitutional methods."*
   - Between 1927 and 1935, Ambedkar organized mass satyagrahas—Mahad (1927) for water rights, and Kalaram Temple Entry Satyagraha at Nashik (1930).
   - Experiencing brutal upper-caste backlash and realizing that temple entry did not alter Dalit economic or social status, Ambedkar abandoned Satyagraha. He concluded that in a caste-ridden society, moral appeals do not move entrenched oppressors; only **state power, legislative statutes, and constitutional law** can enforce equality.
2. **Constitutional Morality vs Grammar of Anarchy**:
   - In his historic final speech to the Constituent Assembly (November 25, 1949), Ambedkar warned:
     > *"If we wish to maintain democracy not merely in form, but also in fact, what must we do? The first thing in my judgement we must do is to hold fast to constitutional methods of achieving our social and economic objectives... where constitutional methods are open, there can be no justification for unconstitutional methods. These methods are nothing but the Grammar of Anarchy and the sooner they are abandoned, the better for us."*
3. **Economic Emancipation**:
   - While Gandhi championed **Trusteeship** (convincing the wealthy capitalist to act as a moral trustee of wealth), Ambedkar in *States and Minorities* (1947) proposed **Constitutional State Socialism**: state ownership of agricultural land, collective farming, state control of basic industries, and compulsory life insurance to prevent both private monopoly and caste tyranny.

### I. Arundhati Roy's *The Doctor and the Saint* & Contemporary Historiographical Interventions

In her extensive 2014 introduction to the annotated critical edition of Ambedkar's *Annihilation of Caste*, titled *The Doctor and the Saint*, Arundhati Roy (cited explicitly on `page_066.png`) synthesizes the modern scholarly re-evaluation of this debate:
1. **De-sanctifying the Saint**:
   - Roy argues that Indian nationalist historiography has canonized Gandhi as the sole "Father of the Nation", reducing Ambedkar to a niche "Dalit icon" or merely the draftsman of the Constitution.
   - Roy unmasks Gandhi's early racial prejudice in South Africa (where he sought separate entrances for Indians to avoid mixing with indigenous Africans/Kaffirs) and his stubborn lifelong defence of the romanticised *Chaturvarnya* system.
2. **The Doctor's Structural Diagnosis**:
   - Ambedkar, the "Doctor", possessed the clinical, rational, and modern analytical tools to diagnose caste as a structural pathology requiring surgery, while Gandhi, the "Saint", applied spiritualised home remedies and moral exhortations that preserved the underlying caste hierarchy.
3. **Complementarity vs Irreconcilability**:
   - While modern liberal scholars like Ramachandra Guha attempt to reconcile both thinkers—viewing Gandhi as the nation-builder of political independence and Ambedkar as the conscience-keeper of social democracy—subaltern and Dalit scholars (Gopal Guru, Kancha Ilaiah) argue that their worldviews remain fundamentally irreconcilable: one cannot embrace Ambedkar's egalitarian annihilation of caste without rejecting Gandhi's romanticization of Varna and the traditional village.

### A. Points of Convergence: Class Exploitation and Working-Class Rights
1. **Influence of Liberalism and Marxism**: Ambedkar was profoundly influenced by both Western Liberalism (individual liberty, rule of law) and Marxism (critique of economic exploitation).
2. **Acceptance of Class Struggle & Private Property Exploitation**: Ambedkar fully accepted Marx's premise that private property generates inequality and exploitation. In his work as Labour Member of the Viceroy's Executive Council (1942–1946), he established the 8-hour workday, maternity benefits, trade union recognition, and the Employment Exchange system.
3. **Founding of the Independent Labour Party (ILP, 1936)**: Ambedkar deliberately organized the ILP around a combined program of working-class struggle against capitalist bosses and anti-caste struggle against landlordism (*Khoti* system).

### B. Divergence 1: Critique of Historical Materialism (Brahmanism as Base Structure)

```
                 MARXIST PARADIGM                                 AMBEDKARITE PARADIGM
         ┌──────────────────────────────┐                 ┌──────────────────────────────┐
         │ SUPERSTRUCTURE               │                 │ SUPERSTRUCTURE               │
         │ (Law, Religion, State, Caste)│                 │ (State Law, Economy, Politics│
         └──────────────▲───────────────┘                 └──────────────▲───────────────┘
                        │ (Determined by)                                │ (Sustained by)
         ┌──────────────┴───────────────┐                 ┌──────────────┴───────────────┐
         │ ECONOMIC BASE                │                 │ IDEOLOGICAL BASE             │
         │ (Forces & Relations of Prod.)│                 │ (Brahmanism, Manuwad, Caste) │
         └──────────────────────────────┘                 └──────────────────────────────┘
```

- **Marx's Historical Materialism**: Marx postulated that the economic substructure (relations of production) determines the socio-cultural and religious superstructure. If you transform the material conditions (overthrow private property), the superstructure (including caste and religion) will automatically wither away.
- **Ambedkar's Inversion for India**:
  - In *Annihilation of Caste*, Ambedkar demonstrated that **Historical Materialism fails completely in the Indian context**.
  - In India, **the basic structure of society is not economic, but ideological—it is the ideology of Brahmanism / Manuwad**.
  - Caste is not a mere reflection of economic class; it determines economic relations. An upper-caste pauper still enjoys social prestige and ritual superiority over an untouchable millionaire or doctor.
  - A material revolution that nationalizes property will leave caste intact: upper-caste proletarians will still refuse to inter-dine or inter-marry with Dalit proletarians.
  - Therefore, without smashing the ideological hegemony of Brahmanism, **no economic restructuring can ever liberate the Dalit**.

---

### C. Divergence 2: Theory of the State (State as Liberator vs Instrument of Evil)
- **Marx's Anti-Statism**: In *The Communist Manifesto* and *The State and Revolution*, Marx and Lenin defined the state as an executive committee and instrument of violence used by the ruling class to oppress the proletariat, which must be smashed and will ultimately "wither away".
- **Ambedkar's Defense of the State**:
  - As handwritten on `page_067.png`: *"Marx held state as an instrument of evil. According to Ambedkar, society is a bigger evil than state. Society can be more exploitative."*
  - For minorities and Dalits, civil society in India is not a sphere of freedom (as in Hegel or Marx); it is a sphere of entrenched, unaccountable caste tyranny and violent social boycott.
  - The State is the only neutral, centralized agency armed with the constitutional authority to pass civil rights laws, enforce reservations (affirmative action), disarm feudal landlord militias, and guarantee legal equality.

---

### D. Divergence 3: Nature of Religion (Nourishment of Soul vs Opium of the Masses)
- **Marx's Materialist Critique**: *"Religion is the sigh of the oppressed creature, the heart of a heartless world... It is the opium of the people."* Marx viewed religion as an ideological narcotic that numbs the pain of class exploitation and diverts revolutionary anger into false hopes of an afterlife.
- **Ambedkar's Rebuttal**:
  - As captured on `page_067.png`: *"Marx has not read all religions... Buddhism is not opium of masses. Buddhism has lot for nourishment of human soul."*
  - Religion is an existential and ethical necessity for human beings; society cannot survive without a shared moral code (*Dhamma*).
  - While Hinduism (Brahmanism) functions like opium—keeping Dalits docile under the curse of Karma—Buddhism is the exact opposite. Buddhism is grounded in reason, human dignity, liberty, equality, and fraternity. In *Buddha or Karl Marx* (1956), Ambedkar noted:
    > *"Marxism can provide bread, but it cannot provide the moral compass for the soul. The Buddha provides both social justice and spiritual enlightenment."*

---

### E. Divergence 4: Constitutional Democracy vs Dictatorship of the Proletariat
- **Marxian Methodology**: The inevitable transition from capitalism to socialism requires a violent proletarian revolution and the establishment of an authoritarian **Dictatorship of the Proletariat** to suppress counter-revolutionaries.
- **Ambedkar's Rejection of Dictatorship**:
  - As recorded on `page_068.png`: *"Ambedkar was a supporter of liberal democracy even for a transitional period he was not ready to accept dictatorship of proletariat."*
  - Ambedkar argued that any dictatorship, once established under the pretext of a "transitional stage", entrenches a permanent totalitarian bureaucracy that destroys human freedom.
  - Democratic means must match democratic ends. Emancipation can only be achieved through parliamentary democracy, constitutional morality, an independent judiciary, fundamental rights, and peaceful political agitation.

### A. Dharmashastra vs. Arthashastra: Bhikhu Parekh's Thesis of Continuity over Change

In comparative Indian political theory, Professor Bhikhu Parekh provides the definitive synthesis:
1. **Continuity of Degree, Not of Kind**:
   - As noted in the handwritten text: *"According to Bhikhu Parekh, there is more of continuity than change. The difference is only of degree rather than kind."*
   - Western scholars often artificially contrast Dharmashastra as "theology" and Arthashastra as "Machiavellian secular realpolitik". Parekh refutes this dichotomy.
   - Both texts share the identical ontological and civilizational worldview of Brahmanical Hinduism:
     - Both accept the **Quasi-Contractual Theory of the Origin of the State**: In both traditions, the state is created to terminate *Matsyanyaya* (the law of the fishes / state of nature) where the strong devour the weak.
     - Both uphold the institution of **Hereditary Monarchy** (*Rajya*).
     - Both agree on the ultimate end of the state: achieving **Yogakshema** (securing the unacquired and protecting the acquired) to enable the subjects to pursue the **Four Purusharthas** (Dharma, Artha, Kama, Moksha).
2. **The Primacy of Caste Order (*Varnashrama Dharma*)**:
   - In both Dharmashastra and Arthashastra, the primary justification for the King's existence and authority is **to prevent Varnasankara (caste intermixture)** and ensure that each varna strictly observes its hereditary duties (*Svadharma*).
   - Even Kautilya, despite his secular, pragmatic calculations, mandates that the King must use Danda to preserve the sacred social hierarchy established by the Vedas and Dharmashastras.

### B. The Dialectics of Dharma and Danda: The Subordination of the King

```
                                    ┌───────────────────────┐
                                    │    RITA / COSMIC LAW   │
                                    └───────────┬───────────┘
                                                │
                                    ┌───────────▼───────────┐
                                    │         DHARMA        │

                                    └───────────┬───────────┘
                                                │
                       ┌────────────────────────┴────────────────────────┐
                       ▼                                                 ▼
             ┌──────────────────┐                              ┌──────────────────┐
             │       KING       │                              │      DANDA       │
             │ (Ruler / Agent)  │ ◄─────────────────────────── │ (Instrument of   │
             │ *Under Dharma*   │   King enforces Dharma via   │  Punishment)     │
             └──────────────────┘   Danda, but Danda strikes   └──────────────────┘

```

1. **The King is NOT Sovereign; Dharma is Sovereign**:
   - In Western political theory (Hobbes, Austin), the King/Sovereign makes the law and is above the law.
   - In ancient Indian political thought, **the King is under Dharma** (*Dharmasya Gopa*). The King does not make the law; he only discovers, interprets, and enforces the law revealed in the *Shrutis* (Vedas) and codified in the *Smritis* (Dharmashastras).
2. **Danda as the Instrument of Dharma**:
   - Danda (force, coercive authority, punishment) is legitimate only when it serves Dharma.
   - As Manu states in the *Manusmriti* (Chapter 7), Danda is a double-edged sword: if the King exercises Danda justly according to Dharma, the kingdom prospers; but if the King exercises Danda arbitrarily or unjustly, **the same Danda will strike down the King and destroy his dynasty**.
3. **The Prevention of Arajakta (Anarchy)**:
   - If Dharma is not maintained through the judicious application of Danda, society collapses into **Arajakta** (*Matsyanyaya*), where moral order dissolves, property is stolen, women are violated, and the caste boundaries are obliterated.

---

### C. The Significance of Dharma in Ancient Political Thought

1. **Architectonic Principle**: Dharma is the master-concept (*architectonic norm*) governing every facet of Indian social, political, ethical, and metaphysical existence.
2. **Etymological Foundations**:
   - Derived from the Sanskrit root *Dhr* (to hold, support, sustain).
   - Concept: *"Prithvim Dharmam Dhritam"*—Dharma is that cosmic and moral adhesive which holds the earth and humanity together.
3. **Cosmic Origin in *Rita* (Rte)**:
   - In Vedic ontology, the source of Dharma is *Rita*—the supreme, impersonal cosmic law governing the seasons, the planetary orbits, and the moral order of the universe. Dharma is the human, institutional application of this cosmic Rita.
4. **Plurality of Dharma**:
   - Dharma is never monolithic; it manifests contextually as:
     - *Samanya Dharma* (universal moral virtues: truth, non-injury, restraint).
     - *Varnashrama Dharma* (duties specific to one's caste and life-stage).
     - *Rajadharma* (the sacred political duty of the King).
     - *Apad-dharma* (exceptional conduct permitted during existential crises).

### B. The Philosophy of Constitutionalism as a Political Strategy

As defined in `page_070.png`:
1. **Rooted in Classical Liberalism**:
   - Constitutionalism is the political strategy of the early Indian nationalists (the Moderates: 1885–1905).
   - It is grounded in the Enlightenment philosophy of Liberalism: human beings are fundamentally rational creatures capable of resolving political conflicts through reason, institutional debate, and peaceful dialogue.
   - Street agitation, mass strikes, and violence are deemed uncivilized and counter-productive.
2. **Gopal Krishna Gokhale's Authoritative Definition (Allahabad, 1907)**:
   - Addressing the 1907 Allahabad session of the Indian National Congress, Gokhale defined constitutional methods:
     > *"Constitutional methods are those methods which are adopted to bring about desired political changes through the action of constituted authorities."*
   - Its primary mechanisms are:
     1. Moving resolutions in the legislative councils.
     2. Submitting carefully reasoned memorials and petitions.
     3. Prayers and appeals to British justice.
     4. Mobilizing enlightened public opinion through the press.
3. **Categorical Exclusion of Direct Action**:
   - Shubhra Ranjan's notes on `page_071.png` provide a comprehensive catalog of methods **strictly excluded** by constitutionalism:
     - Passive Resistance
     - Satyagraha
     - Non-Cooperation
     - Dharna and Hartal
     - Civil Disobedience
     - Boycott
     - Rebellion and Mutiny
     - Revolutionary Terrorism
     - Criminal actions & aiding foreign invasions.

### C. Why Early Nationalists Adopted Constitutional Methods

The rationale of the Moderates is split into two complementary dimensions: **Ideological Reasons** and **Historical/Strategic Reasons**.

#### 1. Ideological Reasons (`page_071.png`)
The early nationalists (Dadabhai Naoroji, Pherozeshah Mehta, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Justice M.G. Ranade, Surendranath Banerjee, Dinshaw Wacha) were thoroughly educated in British liberal thought (John Locke, J.S. Mill, Edmund Burke):
- **British Rule as a "Providential Mission" / "Blessing in Disguise"**:
  - The Moderates viewed the British conquest not as a calamity, but as divine intervention designed to rescue India from the decay, despotism, and social backwardness of the feudal past.
- **Justice Mahadev Govind Ranade**:
  - Publicly thanked the British for introducing the **Rule of Law**, modern education, equality before law, and administrative unification.
  - Ranade declared that British rule was a source of *"inspiration, hope, and confidence"* and that Indians were *"fortunate to be under British rule rather than any other imperialist power"* (such as Tsarist Russia or France).
- **Sir Surendranath Banerjee**:
  - Appealed to the British Raj to introduce institutions that were *"truly British in character so that Indians can rejoice in permanent union with Britain."*
- **Dadabhai Naoroji**:
  - Argued in *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* (1901) that the British people were inherently fair and justice-loving. If India suffered, it was because the British public was ignorant of the rapacity of the colonial bureaucracy. The task of Congress was simply to communicate Indian grievances in a reasoned, constitutional manner to the British Parliament.

#### 2. Historical & Strategic Reasons (`page_072.png`)
Modern nationalist historian **Bipin Chandra** (*India's Struggle for Independence*) provides a brilliant defence of the Moderates, proving that their constitutionalism was not mere cowardice, but a historically necessary stage:
- **Immense Preparation Required (Gokhale)**:
  - Gokhale emphasized that India was not yet ready for direct action. A mass movement against the world's greatest imperial empire requires immense psychological, organizational, and material preparation. Launching premature mass protests would invite brutal state suppression and destroy the infant national movement.
- **Internal Fragmentation & Social Disunity (C.Y. Chintamani)**:
  - Renowned liberal journalist and leader C.Y. Chintamani pointed out that before confronting British imperialism, Indians had to overcome massive internal divisions of caste, religion, language, and region.
- **Bipin Chandra's Thesis: "Leaders as Well as Learners"**:
  - *Objective vs Subjective Conditions*: Colonial rule was objectively uniting India through railways, telegraphs, and common administration. However, the **subjective consciousness** of a shared Indian national identity was completely missing.
  - *Nation-in-the-Making*: India was not an already-formed nation; it was a "nation-in-the-making". The Moderates had to gently nurture this common national identity without triggering state repression.
  - *Moderates as Learners*: There was no pre-existing handbook or readymade critique of modern imperialism anywhere in the world. The Moderates had to study, learn, and expose the exploitative nature of British rule step-by-step (e.g., Naoroji's **Economic Drain Theory**).
  - *Hidden Exploitation*: British imperialism did not look like brutal medieval conquerors; it arrived wearing the modern mask of railways, legal courts, and civil service. The evil features of the empire were not on the surface; they had to be unearthed through rigorous statistical and economic critique.

### D. The Militant & Extremist Critique of Constitutionalism (`page_072.png`)

By the late 1890s and early 1900s, a new generation of militant nationalists—the Extremists / Radicals (Lal-Bal-Pal: Lala Lajpat Rai, Bal Gangadhar Tilak, Bipin Chandra Pal, and Sri Aurobindo Ghosh)—launched a devastating ideological assault on Moderate constitutionalism.

#### 1. Bal Gangadhar Tilak: The Critique of Unconstitutional Despotism
- Tilak argued that constitutional agitation makes sense in Great Britain where the government is formed by the people, answers to Parliament, and is governed by a domestic constitution.
- In India, however, the government is an **unaccountable alien despotism** whose sole purpose is colonial extraction.
- Tilak delivered the historic verdict recorded on `page_072.png`:
  > *"Constitutionalism in front of an alien bureaucracy is political suicide."*
- You cannot appeal to the conscience of a foreign autocracy; political rights are not granted as gifts—**"Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it!"** Tilak introduced direct methods: *Swadeshi*, *Boycott*, *National Education*, and mass mobilization via *Ganeshotsav* and *Shivaji Utsav*.

#### 2. Sri Aurobindo Ghosh: Indictment in *New Lamps for Old* (1893)
- Writing a series of explosive articles in the *Indu Prakash* titled *New Lamps for Old*, young Aurobindo Ghosh tore through the Moderate establishment:
  - He indicted Moderate constitutionalism as the **"intellectual bankruptcy of moderates"**.
  - He characterized Congress politics as a policy of **"political mendicancy"** (holding out a begging bowl to the master).
  - Aurobindo pointed out that the Moderates were an anglicized, isolated upper-middle-class elite who feared their own proletariat. True power, Aurobindo insisted, lay not in British petitions, but in the unorganized, dormant revolutionary energy of the Indian masses (*Shakti*).

#### 3. Pandit Jawaharlal Nehru's Verdict
- Echoing Tilak and Aurobindo, Nehru noted in *The Discovery of India* and *An Autobiography*:
  - Constitutional agitation is a contradiction in terms under a colonial regime.
  - **Constitutional methods are not possible without constitutional governance.** In an autocratic police state, the British administration uses the Indian Penal Code (IPC Sec. 124A - Sedition) to crush any genuine demand; therefore, extra-constitutional mass civil disobedience is the only viable path.

### Model Answer 1 (20 Marks / 250 Words)

> **Question**: *"Annihilation of caste requires not the moral reform of individuals, but the dynamiting of the Shastras." Evaluate Dr. B.R. Ambedkar's critique of Mahatma Gandhi's approach to the caste question.*

#### Model Structure & Content:
- **Introduction**: Introduce the historic polemic between Dr. B.R. Ambedkar and Mahatma Gandhi, framed by Arundhati Roy as the encounter between "The Doctor and the Saint". While Gandhi approached caste as an insider seeking moral repentance, Ambedkar approached it as an Enlightenment radical demanding structural demolition.
- **Gandhi's Insider-Reformist Approach**:
  - Distinguishes between pristine *Varna* (ideal division of labour, non-competitive social harmony) and degenerate *Caste* (*Jati*).
  - Categorizes untouchability as a "sin" (*Kalank*) of the Hindu community requiring moral atonement (*Prayaschitta*) through upper-caste *Seva* (manifested in the Harijan Sevak Sangh).
  - Employs paternal terminology (*Harijan*) and advocates temple entry and moral persuasion.
- **Ambedkar's Radical Critique (Annihilation of Caste, 1936)**:
  - *Textual vs Contextual*: Rebuts Gandhi's textual bookish defense of Varna. In lived reality, Varna is not division of labour, but **a division of labourers into graded water-tight compartments**.
  - *Sanctity of the Shastras*: Rejects Gandhi's moral appeals. Unlike Western racial prejudice, caste discrimination in India is sacred duty commanded by the *Vedas*, *Smritis*, and *Manusmriti*. As long as the scriptures retain authority, the Hindu conscience will treat untouchability as righteousness. Hence, Ambedkar commands: *"You must dynamite the Shastras."*
  - *The Putana Allegory*: Indicts Gandhi's Harijan Sevak Sangh for excluding Dalits from leadership, comparing its upper-caste paternalism to the demoness Putana feeding sweet poison to smother autonomous Dalit militancy.
  - *The Spatial Trap*: Gandhi's *Gram Swaraj* is a "den of localism and ignorance", confining Dalits to ghettoized servitude.
- **Conclusion**: Conclude with Eleanor Zelliot's observation that Gandhi sought to save Hinduism from untouchability, whereas Ambedkar sought to save untouchables from Hinduism. Emancipation required the replacement of sentimental moralism with constitutional morality, rule of law, and state power.

### Model Answer 2 (15 Marks / 200 Words)

> **Question**: *"The basic structure of Indian society is ideological, not economic." In the light of this statement, critically analyze Dr. B.R. Ambedkar's engagement with Marxism.*

#### Model Structure & Content:
- **Introduction**: Cite Ambedkar's 1956 treatise *Buddha or Karl Marx*. While deeply influenced by the Marxist critique of class exploitation, Ambedkar rejected the dogmatic application of Marxist Historical Materialism to Indian socio-political reality.
- **Points of Convergence with Marx**:
  - Ambedkar conceded the exploitative nature of private property, endorsed the reality of class conflict, and championed working-class rights (forming the Independent Labour Party in 1936).
- **The Core Divergence: Inversion of Base and Superstructure**:
  - *Critique of Historical Materialism*: Marx claimed the economic mode of production forms the base, while religion and ideology are mere superstructure. Ambedkar argued that in India, **Brahmanism and caste ideology constitute the true base structure**.
  - *Caste Prevents Class Consciousness*: Caste cuts across economic classes horizontally. A Dalit worker and a Brahmin worker do not share a common proletarian identity; religious pollution prevents collective action.
  - *Futile Materialism*: Transforming the material mode of production without annihilating Brahmanism leaves caste oppression intact, as social status in India is determined by birth (*Jati*), not wealth.
- **State and Religion Divergences**:
  - *State as Protector*: While Marx viewed the state as an instrument of class evil, Ambedkar viewed society as the greater evil and relied on the state for affirmative action and protective discrimination.
  - *Religion as Nourishment*: Refuted Marx's thesis of religion as the "opium of the masses". Religion is an essential moral compass; while Brahmanical Hinduism poisons, Navayana Buddhism nourishes the human soul.
- **Conclusion**: Summarize that Ambedkar synthesized Liberalism, Marxism, and Buddhism: rejecting violent revolution and the dictatorship of the proletariat, he anchored Dalit emancipation in constitutional democracy and ethical humanism.

### Model Answer 3 (15 Marks / 200 Words)

> **Question**: *"Constitutionalism in front of an alien bureaucracy is political suicide." Critically examine the debate between the Moderates and Extremists regarding the methods of the early Indian National Movement.*

#### Model Structure & Content:
- **Introduction**: Attribute the famous quote to Bal Gangadhar Tilak. The transition from the Moderate phase (1885–1905) to the Extremist phase (1905–1919) marked an epistemic shift from liberal constitutionalism to militant mass nationalism.
- **The Moderate Strategy of Constitutionalism**:
  - Grounded in British Liberalism (Mill, Locke) and faith in dialogue.
  - Defined by Gokhale (1907 Allahabad Address) as action through constituted authorities: the **3Ps** (Prayer, Petition, Protest), assembly resolutions, and memorials.
  - *Ideological Rationale*: Belief in British sense of justice (Naoroji) and British rule as a "providential mission" / "blessing in disguise" introducing the Rule of Law (Ranade).
  - *Historical Rationale (Bipin Chandra)*: Moderates were "leaders and learners". In an unformed "nation-in-the-making", premature mass action would invite ruthless state suppression.
- **The Extremist Indictment**:
  - *Tilak's Attack*: Constitutionalism is viable only under a constitutional government with sovereign citizenship. Under an alien colonial despotism, it amounts to "political suicide".
  - *Aurobindo's Critique*: In *New Lamps for Old*, denounced Moderate methods as the "intellectual bankruptcy of moderates" and "political mendicancy".
  - *Nehru's View*: Constitutional agitation is impossible without constitutional governance.
- **Evaluation & Synthesis**:
  - The Extremists injected mass pride, self-reliance, and direct action (*Swadeshi, Boycott, Passive Resistance*).
  - However, Bipin Chandra reminds us that without the Moderates' foundational economic critique (Drain Theory) and pan-Indian institutional apparatus, the Extremist and Gandhian mass phases would have had no ground to stand on.
