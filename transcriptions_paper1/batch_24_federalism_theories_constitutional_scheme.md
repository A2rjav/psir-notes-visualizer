### 1. Neera Chandhoke vs. Andre Beteille on Civil Society
1. **Neera Chandhoke (*State and Civil Society: Explorations in Political Theory*, 1995):**
   - Chandhoke conceptualizes civil society not as an uncritical space of harmony or autonomous virtue, but as a **site of contestation, power asymmetries, and democratic struggle**.
   - Civil society cannot exist in opposition to or in complete independence of the State; rather, civil society is *constituted by the state*, and the state is held accountable by civil society.
   - Without the institutional guarantee of rights, rule of law, and democratic freedoms provided by the State, civil society descends into an arena where dominant castes, patriarchal structures, and capital suppress marginalized voices.
   - She cautions against the neo-liberal "de-statisation" discourse that attempts to substitute NGOs and civil society organisations for state welfare responsibilities.

2. **Andre Beteille (*Civil Society and Its Institutions*, 2001):**
   - Beteille offers a sociological, Weberian formulation: Civil society consists of **open, secular, and rule-bound institutions** that stand between the individual and the State (universities, professional associations, trade unions, free press, judiciary).
   - In India, Beteille warns against equating primordial associations (caste sabhas, religious trusts, kinship networks) with "civil society." Traditional Indian society was organized around closed, ascriptive hierarchies; authentic civil society requires citizenship-based, voluntary, and universalist norms.

### 2. Typology of Social Movements in Post-Independence India
1. **Old Social Movements (Materialist / Class-Based):**
   - Focused on economic redistribution, state capture, ownership of the means of production, wages, and land tenancy.
   - *Peasant Movements:* Telengana (1946–51), Tebhaga (1946), Naxalbari (1967); later transformed into rich farmers' mobilizations (Sharad Joshi's Shetkari Sanghatana, Mahendra Singh Tikait's Bharatiya Kisan Union) fighting for remunerative prices, input subsidies, and loan waivers (*"Bharat vs. India"* dichotomy).
   - *Labour / Trade Union Movements:* Historically aligned with major political parties (AITUC with CPI, INTUC with Congress, CITU with CPM, BMS with RSS/BJP); heavily weakened post-1991 liberalization due to contractualization and the informalization of the workforce.
2. **New Social Movements (Post-Materialist / Identity & Rights-Based):**
   - Driven by identity, ecological sustainability, human dignity, bodily integrity, and democratic governance rather than raw capture of state power (Gail Omvedt, Rajni Kothari's *"Non-Party Political Formations"*).
   - *Ecological Movements:* Chipko, Narmada Bachao Andolan (Medha Patkar), protesting "development-induced displacement" and state ecological violence.
   - *Women's Movement:* Anti-dowry agitation, Mathura rape case mobilization (leading to Criminal Law Amendments), Campaign against domestic violence, struggle for political reservation (106th Amendment Act / Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam).
   - *Anti-Corruption & Democratic Renewal:* India Against Corruption (IAC, 2011) led by Anna Hazare; culminated in the birth of the Aam Aadmi Party (AAP) representing an anti-party, grassroots swaraj political formation.

#### Comprehensive PSIR Scholarly Commentary: Page 171 (Page 84)

##### 1. Corporate Groups & Economic Diplomacy (Point v)
- Post-1991 LPG reforms (Liberalisation, Privatisation, Globalisation) fundamentally restructured the state-business interface.
- Peak business associations—such as the **Confederation of Indian Industry (CII)**, **Federation of Indian Chambers of Commerce and Industry (FICCI)**, and **ASSOCHAM**—have evolved from defensive lobbying entities seeking import licenses (under the "License-Permit-Quota Raj") to active partners in economic diplomacy.
- Corporate delegations routinely accompany the Prime Minister and Head of State on foreign bilateral visits. Parallel "CEO Forums" and international business conclaves (e.g., India-US CEO Forum, Pravasi Bharatiya Divas business meets, World Economic Forum at Davos) operate synchronously with official political diplomacy.
- **Sanjaya Baru** (*The Accidental Prime Minister*, *India's Power Elite*) highlights how domestic corporate conglomerates have become critical stakeholders in external geo-economic negotiations and trade agreements.

##### 2. Asymmetries within Civil Society & Pressure Group Politics (Points vi & vii)
- **Communal Organisations:** The continued resilience and institutional penetration of faith-based and communal organisations reflects what **Christophe Jaffrelot** terms the *institutionalization of ethno-nationalism* in public life.
- **Partisan Alignment of Mass Fronts:** Student unions (ABVP, NSUI, SFI, AISF) and trade unions (BMS, INTUC, CITU, AITUC) remain subordinate adjuncts or "transmission belts" of mainstream electoral parties, curtailing their autonomy as pure, independent pressure groups.
- **Weak Influence of Gender and Ecological Lobbies:** Despite visible mobilizations, women's groups and environmental NGOs struggle to exercise sustained, structural leverage over legislative policy because they rarely operate as cohesive, identity-based vote-banks.
- **Agrarian Lobby Skew:** State policy consistently prioritizes the interests of the *kulak class* or rich, surplus-producing farmers (subsidized electricity, fertilizer subsidies, Minimum Support Price procurement for wheat and paddy) over the acute structural crises faced by marginal farmers and agricultural laborers. **Rudolph & Rudolph** (*In Pursuit of Lakshmi: The Political Economy of the Indian State*) characterize these surplus farmers as "bullock capitalists" who exercise disproportionate electoral leverage over rural constituencies.

##### 3. The Hardgrave & Kochanek Paradigm: From State-Dominated Pluralism to Autonomous Pressure Politics
- In *India: Government and Politics in a Developing Nation*, **Robert L. Hardgrave and Stanley A. Kochanek** mapped the structural transformation of India's interest articulation regime:
  - **Nehruvian Phase ("State-Dominated Pluralism"):** The developmental state occupied the "commanding heights" of both the economy and political discourse. Interest groups were weak, fragmented, heavily penetrated by the Congress system, and functioned as supplicants to state planners.
  - **Post-1990s Transition:** The retreating regulatory state, coalition politics, economic decentralization, and the explosion of regional/corporate interests dismantled state hegemony. Business lobbies, agrarian coalitions, and assertive caste/regional federations now possess autonomous veto power, moving India toward robust, multi-centric pressure group politics.

#### Comprehensive PSIR Scholarly Commentary: Page 172

##### 1. Epistemological Justification for Studying Legislative Profiles
- **Descriptive Representation (Hanna Pitkin, *The Concept of Representation*):** Parliament must descriptively reflect the composition of the nation. When marginalized social groups (women, lower castes, religious minorities) are absent, the legislative body suffers from a deficit of democratic legitimacy.
- **Direction of Political Development:** Tracing shifts in occupational, educational, age, and caste profiles provides empirical evidence of structural power shifts in the broader political sociology of India (e.g., transition from feudal/patrician hegemony to plebeian and vernacular democracy).

##### 2. The Bicameral Paradox in India: "Peter Drunk vs. Peter Sober"
- The notes cite the classic Anglo-Saxon constitutional aphorism attributed to John Selden/English jurisprudence:
  $$\text{Lower House (Lok Sabha)} = \text{"Peter Drunk" (Populist passion, immediate electoral frenzy)}$$
  $$\text{Upper House (Rajya Sabha)} = \text{"Peter Sober" (Deliberative wisdom, cool second reflection, Assembly of Elders)}$$
- **Indian Reality:** In practice, the empirical profiles of Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha members exhibit no significant socio-demographic divergence. Both houses mirror identical socio-economic strata. Furthermore, the abolition of the residential qualification for Rajya Sabha through the *Representation of the People (Amendment) Act, 2003* (upheld by the Supreme Court in *Kuldip Nayar v. Union of India*, 2006) turned the Upper House into a chamber for accommodating party apparatchiks and defeated electoral politicians, rather than a genuine federal council of states.

##### 3. Shanker & Rodrigues' Landmark Study: Phase I (Uptill 1967)
- **Scholars:** B.L. Shankar & Valerian Rodrigues (*The Indian Parliament: A Democracy at Work*, Oxford University Press, 2011).
- **Phase I Characteristics (1952–1967 - The Nehruvian Parliament):**
  - *Social Stratification:* Marked by upper-caste (predominantly Brahmin and Kayastha) hegemony. Over 60% of the early Lok Sabhas belonged to forward castes.
  - *Severe Under-representation:* Women hovered at 4.4% in the 1st Lok Sabha (1952); Other Backward Classes (OBCs) and Muslims were marginalized.
  - *Occupational Dominance:* The legal fraternity constituted the single largest occupational bloc (comprising over 30% of the 1st Lok Sabha), creating what Sir Ivor Jennings famously critiqued as a "Lawyer's Paradise" reflected in the verbose, hyper-legalistic style of parliamentary debate.
  - *Educational Background:* Dominated by Western-educated elites holding Oxbridge, London School of Economics, or Inns of Court credentials. Even Scheduled Caste legislators elected via reserved seats (e.g., Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Jagjivan Ram) held advanced higher degrees from elite institutions abroad or in presidency capitals.

##### 1. Phase II (1967–1990s): The Transitional Zone & First Democratic Upsurge
- **Structural Catalysts:**
  - *Green Revolution:* Transformed the rural economic structure across Northwestern and Western India, producing a prosperous class of peasant landowners (Jats, Yadavs, Kurmis, Marathas, Vokkaligas, Lingayats).
  - *Linguistic State Reorganisation:* Empowered intermediate regional castes who lacked fluent English but exercised formidable numerical dominance in vernacular rural politics.
- **Yogendra Yadav's "First Democratic Upsurge":**
  - Yadav characterizes the late 1960s and 1970s as the entry of the rural peasantry into the democratic arena. The 1967 General Elections shattered the "Congress System" (Rajni Kothari), installing Samyukta Vidhayak Dal (SVD) coalition governments across Northern India.
- **Occupational & Generational Mutation:**
  - Agrarian backgrounds overtook lawyers as the dominant occupational category in the Lok Sabha.
  - "Social Work" and "Full-Time Politics" emerged as professional vocations.
  - **Youth Mobilization:** The Jayaprakash Narayan (JP) Movement against authoritarian centralism (1974–75) and Sanjay Gandhi's aggressive expansion of the Indian Youth Congress inducted a new cohort of student leaders directly into electoral politics at their formative career stage (e.g., Lalu Prasad Yadav, Nitish Kumar, Sharad Yadav, Ram Vilas Paswan).
- **The Stagnation Paradox:**
  - Women's presence stagnated at ~8%. Minority representation showed zero autonomous upward mobility.
  - *Constitutional Proof:* The data conclusively proves that without mandatory affirmative action (Articles 330 and 332 providing political reservation for SCs and STs in proportion to their population), electoral politics in India structurally fails to produce descriptive representation for excluded communities.

##### 2. Phase III (1990s Onwards): Plebeianisation & The Second Democratic Upsurge
- **Concept of Plebeianisation:** Derived from classical Rome (*plebeians* vs. *patricians*), denoting the political enfranchisement, cultural assertiveness, and institutional entry of the common, subaltern, and working-class masses into sovereign state organs.
- **Christophe Jaffrelot's *India's Silent Revolution: The Rise of the Lower Castes in North India* (2003):**
  - Details the transfer of power from the traditional upper-caste English-speaking elite to the Other Backward Classes, catalyzed by the implementation of the Mandal Commission recommendations (1990).
- **Yogendra Yadav's "Second Democratic Upsurge":**
  - The 1990s witnessed an unprecedented surge in voter participation among SCs, STs, and OBCs, surpassing the turnout rates of the urban upper-caste middle class.
  - This upsurge permanently entrenched vernacular politics, regional caste-based parties (SP, BSP, RJD, JD(U)), and federal coalition governments.
  - However, Jaffrelot and Rodrigues note that this plebeianisation remained heavily gender-skewed and patriarchal, failing to dismantle the historical exclusion of women and religious minorities.

##### 1. The Non-Inclusivity of Indian Democracy
- While India is hailed as the world's most populous democracy, the persistent exclusion of women (averaging 8–12% of Lok Sabha seats despite constituting 48.5% of the electorate) demonstrates what **Nivedita Menon** (*Seeing Like a Feminist*) terms the *deep-seated patriarchal resistance* of party political machines.
- The passage of the **Constitution (106th Amendment) Act, 2023** (*Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam*), reserving 33% of seats in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies for women, was enacted precisely to break this structural barrier, although its implementation is contingent upon post-census delimitation.

1. **Educational Paradox:**

2. **Generational Gerontocracy:**
   - Despite India possessing the world's largest young demographic dividend (median age ~28 years), parliamentary leadership remains concentrated in the hands of individuals aged 55 and above.
3. **High Turnover Rate (58% First-Timers):**
   - Reflects the intensity of anti-incumbency at the individual constituency level, forcing parties to regularly replace sitting candidates.
4. **The Crisis of Criminalisation in Politics:**
   - **Milan Vaishnav (*When Crime Pays: Money and Muscle in Indian Politics*, Yale University Press, 2017):**
     - Explains why candidates with criminal backgrounds are selected by political parties and elected by voters.
     - *Party Logic:* Criminal candidates are self-financing; they possess substantial liquid capital to fund skyrocketing campaign expenditures in an era of opaque electoral financing.
     - *Voter Logic:* In regions where state capacity is weak and public service delivery has collapsed, candidates with "muscle power" are perceived as effective local patrons who can enforce justice, resolve neighborhood disputes, and extract resources from a corrupt bureaucracy on behalf of their community (*"theRobin Hood effect"*).
   - **Law Commission of India (244th Report on Electoral Disqualifications):** Recommended fast-track disqualification upon framing of charges for heinous offenses to sanitize representative institutions.

#### Comprehensive PSIR Scholarly Commentary: Page 175 (Page 86)

##### 1. Methodological Approaches to Indian Electoral Behaviour
- **The Behavioural Revolution:** Shifted political inquiry away from formal constitutional-legal arrangements toward empirical, quantitative, and sociological drivers of voter choice (survey research, ecological regression, exit polls).
- **Milan Vaishnav's Heterogeneity Critique:** India's continental scale, cross-cutting cleavages of caste, sub-caste (*jati*), language, religion, and regional party systems make unified national theories of voting behavior empirically elusive.

##### 2. Kenneth Arrow's Impossibility Theorem & Social Choice Theory
- In *Social Choice and Individual Values* (1951), Nobel laureate **Kenneth Arrow** mathematically proved that under any ranked voting system, when voters have three or more distinct options, it is impossible to convert individual preference orderings into an aggregate, transitive community-wide preference without violating basic democratic criteria (unrestricted domain, non-dictatorship, Pareto efficiency, and independence of irrelevant alternatives).
- *Application to Indian Multi-Party Democracy:* In multi-cornered electoral contests with fragmented party options, election outcomes frequently produce aggregated results that do not reflect a coherent, singular majoritarian "will of the people."

##### 3. Mukulika Banerjee's Ethnographic Masterpiece: *Why India Votes* (2014)
- **The Central Paradox:** In mature Western democracies, voter turnout correlates positively with wealth, education, and social status. In India, the reverse holds true: the poorest, least educated, rural citizens vote in significantly higher proportions than the urban, affluent middle class. Why do poor Indians vote enthusiastically when election after election fails to deliver basic public goods, clean drinking water, quality schooling, or healthcare?
- **Banerjee's Fieldwork Findings:**
  1. **"Power Inversion" as Democratic Ritual:** Polling day is experienced as an egalitarian carnival and ritual inversion of daily hierarchies. For one single day, the elite politician and feudal landlord are reduced to humble petitioners seeking the thumb-impression or vote of the untouchable or impoverished laborer.
  2. **Accountability via Dismissal:** Voting is not seen as an affirmative choice of an ideological messiah, but as the constitutional instrument to periodically "dismiss" arrogant rulers.
  3. **Voting as Retribution / Revenge:** An outlet for suppressed popular resentment, enabling the subaltern electorate to punish corrupt or neglectful legislators.

### The 11 Key UPSC PSIR Questions Framed: Complete Analytical Breakdown

Below is the exhaustive structural, theoretical, and constitutional breakdown of the 11 key question areas mapped directly from Page 179:

---

#### Question 1: Nature of Indian Federalism
> *"Analyse the nature of Indian Federalism."*

##### 1. Constitutional Architecture
- **Article 1:** India is characterized as a **"Union of States"** rather than a "Federation of States." Dr. B.R. Ambedkar explained in the Constituent Assembly that this phrase was deliberately chosen to indicate two immutable facts:
  1. The Indian federation is not the result of an agreement among sovereign units (unlike the United States).
  2. The constituent states have no right to secede from the Union (*"The federation is an indestructible Union composed of destructible States"*).

##### 2. Competing Scholarly Conceptualizations
- **K.C. Wheare (*Federal Government*):** Designates India as **"Quasi-Federal"**—a unitary state with subsidiary federal features rather than a federal state with subsidiary unitary features.
- **Granville Austin (*The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation*):** Conceptualizes India as the prime example of **"Cooperative Federalism"**, designed to produce a strong central government without rendering regional governments weak or administratively subservient.
- **W.H. Morris-Jones (*The Government and Politics of India*):** Terms it **"Bargaining Federalism"**, where the Centre and States continually negotiate political and developmental concessions.
- **Paul Appleby:** Describes the Indian system as *"extremely federal, but functioning centrally during times of national emergency"*.
- **Ivor Jennings:** Notes that India is a *"federation with a strong centralizing tendency"*.
- **Sujit Choudhry, Madhav Khosla & Pratap Bhanu Mehta (*The Oxford Handbook of the Indian Constitution*):** Emphasize **Asymmetrical Federalism**—the constitutional recognition of distinct cultural, historical, and geopolitical differences (Article 370 formerly, Articles 371A–J, and the 5th and 6th Schedules).
- **Judicial Verdict (*S.R. Bommai v. Union of India*, 1994):** A 9-judge Constitution bench definitively ruled that **Federalism is part of the Basic Structure** of the Indian Constitution.

---

#### Question 2: From Cooperative to Competitive-Cooperative Federalism
> *"Do you think we are moving from cooperative federalism to competitive-cooperative federalism?"*

##### 1. Classical Cooperative Federalism
- Rooted in the post-independence planning era where the Centre acted as the patriarchal benefactor and States were dependent executioners of centrally funded five-year plans.
- Institutionalized through collaborative bodies: National Development Council (NDC), Inter-State Council (Article 263), and central grant-in-aid transfers under Article 275.

##### 2. The Emergence of "Competitive Federalism"
- Catalyzed by the 1991 economic reforms: States were forced to compete directly for private, foreign direct investment (FDI), and domestic capital.
- **NITI Aayog's Performance Indices:** Annual rankings of states on health, education, sustainable development goals (SDG India Index), ease of doing business, and clean water (*Water Composite Management Index*).
- **The Concept of "Competitive-Cooperative Federalism":** Coined by the 14th Finance Commission and operationalized by NITI Aayog:
  - *Cooperative Axis:* Enhanced unconditional tax devolution (42% under 14th FC, 41% under 15th FC); abolition of discretionary Planning Commission grants; collective decision-making in the **Goods and Services Tax (GST) Council** (Article 279A).
  - *Competitive Axis:* States competing against one another to provide superior infrastructure, deregulated labor laws, transparent land acquisition, and investor-friendly industrial policies.

##### 3. Critical PSIR Assessment
- **Suhas Palshikar & Louise Tillin:** Point out the dark underbelly of competitive federalism: It risks exacerbating regional inequalities between advanced industrial-coastal states (Maharashtra, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu) and underdeveloped hinterland states (Bihar, UP, Odisha), which lack structural capacity to attract private capital.
- **Centralizing Tendencies in Practice:** The proliferation of Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS) with conditional matching grants, terms-of-reference disputes in the Finance Commission, and aggressive central agency interventions challenge genuine cooperative federalism.

---

##### 1. Legislative Relations (Part XI, Articles 245–255)
- **Concurrent List Dominance (Article 254):** In cases of repugnancy between Union and State laws on List III, the Union law prevails automatically. The Centre frequently passes legislation on subjects deeply impacting states without genuine stakeholder consultation (e.g., Farm Acts 2020 on agriculture and trade).
- **Rajya Sabha's Overriding Powers (Article 249):** Power of Parliament to legislate on a State List subject in the "national interest" by a 2/3rd majority vote.
- **Residuary Powers (Article 248):** Vested exclusively in Parliament (unlike the US and Australian federations where residuary powers belong to the constituent states).

##### 2. Administrative Relations (Articles 256–263)
- **Executive Directions (Articles 256 & 257):** State executive power must comply with Union directives; failure triggers **Article 365** (deeming it a failure of constitutional machinery, justifying Article 356).
- **Misuse of Article 356:** Historical weaponization of President's Rule by ruling parties in New Delhi to topple democratically elected opposition state governments (invoked over 100 times prior to *S.R. Bommai*, 1994).
- **All India Services (Article 312):** IAS and IPS officers are recruited and trained by the Centre but serve in state administrations, creating dual control tensions (e.g., central deputation conflicts between MHA and state chief ministers).
- **Central Agencies:** Friction over expanding the jurisdiction of the Border Security Force (BSF), CBI investigations without state "general consent," and Enforcement Directorate (ED) actions.

##### 3. Financial Relations (Part XII, Articles 268–293)
- **Vertical Fiscal Imbalance:** The Centre possesses the most buoyant, elastic tax bases (Corporation Tax, Customs, Income Tax), while States bear the overwhelming burden of socio-economic developmental expenditure (~60% of total state-union expenditure).
- **Surcharges and Cesses (Article 271):** The Union increasingly raises revenues via cesses (e.g., Road & Infrastructure Cess, Health & Education Cess) which are excluded from the divisible tax pool, systematically depriving States of their legitimate share.
- **GST Council Tensions:** Loss of state fiscal autonomy; disputes over GST compensation cess cessations and delayed revenue reimbursements.

#### Question 4: Impact of Globalisation and Changing Party Systems on Federalism
> *"Discuss the changing trend of Indian federalism because of globalisation and changing party system."*

##### 1. Impact of the Changing Party System (Balveer Arora & Rekha Saxena)
1. **The "Congress System" (1947–1967 - Rajni Kothari):** One-party dominant federalism; intergovernmental disputes were resolved within internal Congress party working committees rather than through formal constitutional forums.
2. **Confrontational Centralization (1967–1989):** Rise of regional parties; Indira Gandhi's hyper-centralized governance style and rampant misuse of Article 356 to dismiss state ministries.
3. **Multi-Party Coalition Era (1989–2014 - "Federalisation of the Party System"):** Regional parties (DMK, AIADMK, TDP, TMC, SP, BSP, BJD) became kingmakers in the Lok Sabha. The Centre could not survive without state coalition partners, leading to unprecedented political bargaining power for regional leaders and deep respect for federal autonomy.
4. **Dominant Party Realignment (2014–Present):** Return of a single-party majority under the BJP. Revitalization of centralized national narratives, push for "One Nation, One Election" (Simultaneous Elections), and uniform national policies.

##### 2. Impact of Globalisation (Post-1991)
- **"Para-Diplomacy" / Sub-National Diplomacy:** States directly engage with foreign governments, multinational corporations, and international financial institutions (World Bank, ADB) for loans and investments. Chief Ministers lead global investment summits (Vibrant Gujarat, Magnetic Maharashtra).
- **Decentralization of Economic Power:** The dismantling of central industrial licensing empowered states with superior human capital and infrastructure, redrawing the economic map of the country.

---

#### Question 5: Article 3 and the Destruction of State Boundaries
> *"Do you think article 3 of Indian constitution is non federal feature. Substantiate your views."*

##### 1. Constitutional Text & Mechanism of Article 3
- Parliament may by simple majority law:
  1. Form a new State by separation of territory from any State, by uniting two or more States, or by uniting any territory to a part of any State;
  2. Increase or diminish the area of any State;
  3. Alter the boundaries or name of any State.
- **The Proviso to Article 3:** The President must refer the bill to the concerned State Legislature for expressing its views within a specified period.
- **Critical Caveat:** The views of the State Legislature **are NOT binding** on Parliament (*Babulal Parate v. State of Bombay*, 1960). Parliament can ignore unanimous state legislative resolutions rejecting partition or boundary alterations.

##### 2. Non-Federal Character Substantiated
- **Indestructible Union of Destructible States:** In authentic classical federations (such as the United States under Article IV, Section 3 of the US Constitution), the territorial integrity and boundaries of constituent states cannot be altered without the explicit consent of the state legislatures concerned.
- In India, constituent states do not enjoy guaranteed territorial existence. The Union can wipe a state off the political map, downgrade a state into a Union Territory (e.g., Jammu & Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019), or bifurcate it against the will of its elected assembly (e.g., Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2014).
- **Conclusion:** Article 3 is an emphatically **unitary / non-federal feature**, intentionally introduced by the Constituent Assembly to preserve national unity and enable rational internal territorial reorganization in a fragile post-partition nation.

---

#### Question 6: Politics of State Formation in India
> *"Discuss the politics of state formation in India."*

##### 1. Historical Evolution of Internal Reorganisation
1. **The Linguistic Phase (1950s):**
   - Initial reluctance of the Dhar Commission (1948) and JVP Committee (Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel, Pattabhi Sitaramayya, 1948) to create states on linguistic lines due to balkanization fears.
   - Potti Sreeramulu's 58-day fast unto death leading to the creation of Andhra State (1953).
   - **States Reorganisation Commission (SRC 1953–55 - Fazal Ali, K.M. Panikkar, H.N. Kunzru):** Recognized language as the legitimate basis of internal administrative reorganisation, implemented via the **States Reorganisation Act, 1956** and the 7th Constitutional Amendment Act.
2. **The Geopolitical & Homeland Security Phase (1960s–1970s):**
   - Division of bilingual Bombay State into Gujarat and Maharashtra (1960).
   - Bifurcation of Punjab into Punjab and Haryana (1966).
   - Reorganisation of the strategic North-East to satisfy ethnic and tribal homelands (Nagaland 1963, Meghalaya, Manipur, Tripura 1972, Mizoram, Arunachal Pradesh 1987).
3. **The Administrative & Regional Backwardness Phase (2000):**
   - Creation of Chhattisgarh (from MP), Uttarakhand (from UP), and Jharkhand (from Bihar) based on developmental neglect, administrative non-viability of oversized states, and tribal aspirations.
4. **The Sub-Regional Identity Phase (2014):**
   - Carving out of Telangana from Andhra Pradesh after decades of agitation over regional disparities in irrigation, employment, and political power.

##### 1. Arguments Supporting Smaller States
- **Administrative Accessibility:** Citizens in oversized states (e.g., Uttar Pradesh with 240M+ people) are physically and administratively alienated from distant state capitals.
- **Empirical Governance Record:** States like Haryana and Himachal Pradesh demonstrated that smaller units can achieve superior per-capita income, literacy, and infrastructural connectivity compared to their parent composite states.
- **Deepening Grassroots Democracy:** Better alignment between elected representatives and local populations; more focused deployment of developmental funds.

##### 2. Arguments Against Unchecked Proliferation
- **Fiscal Non-Viability:** Many newly created smaller states lack robust independent tax revenue bases, remaining permanently dependent on Central "Special Category" grants and revenue deficit subsidies.
- **Political Instability:** Smaller legislative assemblies (e.g., 40–60 seats) make governments exceptionally vulnerable to horse-trading, defection of 2–3 MLAs, and frequent political crises.
- **Inter-State Conflict:** Creation of smaller states often unleashes acrimonious disputes over common capitals (e.g., Chandigarh, Hyderabad), river water sharing, and asset division.
- **Scholarly Balance (Atul Kohli, Ashutosh Varshney):** State reorganization is desirable when rooted in genuine administrative and developmental neglect, but dangerous when driven purely by opportunistic political elites creating fiefdoms for personal patronage.

#### Question 8: Controversy of Governor's Office & Landmark Supreme Court Judgments
> *"Why the post of governor remains controversial. Discuss prominent judgements of SC w.r.t institution of governor."*

##### 1. Root Causes of Constitutional Friction
- **Dual Role of the Governor:**
  1. Constitutional Head of the State (acting on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers under Article 163).
  2. Agent / Representative of the Union Government (appointed by the President under Article 155, serving during the "pleasure of the President" under Article 156(1)).
- **Discretionary Flashpoints:**
  - Invoking Article 356 recommending President's Rule.
  - Appointment of the Chief Minister in a hung assembly.
  - Floor test delays and sanctioning prosecution of ministers.
  - Indefinite withholding of assent to state bills under Article 200 or reserving them for the President under Article 201 (*"Pocket Veto"*).

##### 2. Landmark Supreme Court Jurisprudence
1. ***S.R. Bommai v. Union of India* (1994):**
   - Ruled that the power under Article 356 is an exceptional power subject to **full judicial review**.
   - The assembly cannot be dissolved until Parliament approves the presidential proclamation.
   - The **floor of the Legislative Assembly is the only forum** to test the majority of a ministry, stripping the Governor of arbitrary subjective discretion.
2. ***Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India* (2006) (Bihar Assembly Case):**
   - The Governor cannot recommend dissolution of an assembly merely on subjective assessment of political parties horse-trading before a government is formed; declared the Governor's action malafide and unconstitutional.
3. ***B.P. Singhal v. Union of India* (2010):**
   - Addressed the arbitrary removal of Governors following a change of government at the Centre.
   - Ruled that while the President can remove a Governor without assigning reasons, this power **cannot be exercised arbitrarily, capriciously, or on political grounds**. Such removal is open to judicial review.
4. ***Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker* (2016) (Arunachal Pradesh Case):**
   - Clarified that the Governor’s discretionary powers under Article 163 are extremely limited. The Governor cannot summon, prorogue, or dissolve the legislative assembly without the aid and advice of the elected Council of Ministers.
5. ***State of Punjab v. Principal Secretary to Governor* (2023):**
   - The Supreme Court held that the Governor cannot sit indefinitely on bills passed by the legislature; the Governor must either grant assent, return the bill with a message, or reserve it for the President in a time-bound manner, warning Governors not to act as political roadblocks.

#### Question 9: Zonal Councils and Their Contemporary Relevance
> *"Write short note on zonal council and their relevance."*

##### 1. Origin and Statutory Foundation
- Zonal Councils are **statutory bodies**, NOT constitutional bodies.
- Established under **Part III of the States Reorganisation Act, 1956** to promote emotional integration, inter-state cooperation, and mitigate linguistic friction post-reorganisation.
- **Structure:** 5 Zonal Councils (Northern, Central, Eastern, Western, and Southern). The North-Eastern Council (NEC) was created separately under the *North-Eastern Council Act, 1971*.
- **Composition:** The **Union Home Minister acts as the common Chairman** for all Zonal Councils; Chief Ministers of member states serve as Vice-Chairman by annual rotation.

##### 2. Contemporary Relevance
- Act as deliberative, consensus-building inter-governmental forums for resolving regional issues: sharing of river waters, coordinated policing against left-wing extremism and narcotics, power-grid distribution, and coastal security.
- Unlike judicial tribunals which are adversarial, Zonal Councils provide an informal, administrative platform for horizontal (State-State) and vertical (Centre-State) consensus building.

---

#### Question 10: Inter-State Council (Article 263) and Its Institutional Inertia
> *"Short note on interstate council and their relevance."*

##### 1. Constitutional Foundation (Article 263)
- Empowers the President to establish an Inter-State Council to:
  1. Inquire into and advise upon disputes between states;
  2. Investigate and discuss subjects in which some or all of the States, or the Union and one or more States, have a common interest;
  3. Make recommendations for better coordination of policy and action.
- **Establishment:** Set up in **1990** by presidential order on the recommendation of the **Sarkaria Commission (1988)**.
- **Composition:** Prime Minister (Chairman), Chief Ministers of all States and UTs, and 6 Union Cabinet Ministers.

##### 2. Institutional Paralysis & Need for Revitalisation
- **The Problem:** The Inter-State Council has been severely under-utilized, meeting only 11 times in over 30 years (despite the constitutional intention of meeting at least thrice annually).
- **The Punchhi Commission (2010)** strongly recommended transforming the Inter-State Council into a permanent, credible dispute-resolution body with an independent secretariat, taking over functions currently monopolized by ad-hoc central executive diktats.

##### 1. Constitutional & Statutory Framework
- **Article 262:** Empowers Parliament to provide for the adjudication of any dispute with respect to the use, distribution, or control of the waters of any inter-state river or river valley.
- **Exclusion of Supreme Court Jurisdiction (Article 262(2)):** Parliament enacted the **Inter-State River Water Disputes (ISRWD) Act, 1956**, expressly barring the Supreme Court and all courts from entertaining suits or appeals regarding inter-state water disputes referred to a tribunal.
- **The Reality of Judicialization:** Despite Article 262(2), states routinely bypass the bar by filing **Special Leave Petitions (SLPs) under Article 136** or invoking fundamental rights under Article 21 (right to clean water and livelihood), rendering tribunal awards non-final and subjecting them to decades of protracted litigation.

##### 2. Critique of the Tribunal System
- **Extreme Delays:** Ad-hoc tribunals take decades to deliver final awards (Cauvery Tribunal took 17 years; Ravi & Beas Tribunal set up in 1986 remains unresolved).
- **Politicization & Defiance:** Because river water is tied to agrarian survival and local electoral emotions, state governments openly defy tribunal awards and pass contradictory state laws.
- **Lack of Institutional Continuity:** Ad-hoc composition, absence of permanent hydrological data repositories, and lack of enforcement mechanisms.

##### 3. Case Studies

1. **Northern Case Study: Sutlej-Yamuna Link (SYL) Canal Dispute (Punjab vs. Haryana vs. Rajasthan):**
   - Rooted in the 1966 reorganisation of Punjab and subsequent water allocations from the Ravi and Beas rivers (1976 central allocation, 1981 agreement).
   - The Eradi Tribunal was constituted in 1986. Haryana completed its portion of the SYL canal, while Punjab halted construction citing severe groundwater depletion.
   - In 2004, the Punjab Assembly passed the *Punjab Termination of Agreements Act*, unilaterally abrogating all water agreements. The Supreme Court declared this act unconstitutional in a presidential reference (2016), yet political deadlock and non-implementation persist.

2. **Southern Case Study: Cauvery River Water Dispute (Tamil Nadu vs. Karnataka):**
   - Century-old dispute originating from the 1892 and 1924 agreements between the Madras Presidency and the princely state of Mysore.
   - The Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal (CWDT) was established in 1990 and gave its final award in 2007.
   - Karnataka repeatedly contested the award during distress water years (monsoon deficits). Riots, bandhs, and non-compliance ensued.
   - The Supreme Court delivered a historic judgment in 2018, modifying the CWDT award under Article 136, declaring inter-state rivers as **national assets**, and directing the establishment of the **Cauvery Water Management Authority (CWMA)**.

##### 4. Reforms: The Inter-State River Water Disputes (Amendment) Bill
- Proposes a **single, permanent Tribunal** with multiple benches to end ad-hoc delays.
- Mandatory creation of a **Dispute Resolution Committee (DRC)** for pre-tribunal negotiated settlements within a strict 1-year timeline.
- Establishment of a National Geo-Hydrological Data Bank to eliminate partisan disputes over raw water data.

#### Question 12: Centre-State Reforms Without Altering the Basic Structure
> *"Without altering the basic structure of const suggest reforms in the centre-state relations to meet aspirations of Indians in 21st century."*

To modernize Indian federalism without infringing upon the Basic Structure doctrine (*Kesavananda Bharati*, 1973; *S.R. Bommai*, 1994), actionable institutional reforms recommended by the **Sarkaria Commission (1988)**, the **Punchhi Commission (2010)**, and contemporary scholars include:

1. **Reforming the Governor's Appointment and Dismissal (Punchhi Commission):**
   - Amend Article 155 to mandate a selection panel comprising the Prime Minister, Union Home Minister, Speaker of Lok Sabha, and the concerned State Chief Minister.
   - Remove the "pleasure doctrine" (Article 156); grant Governors a fixed 5-year tenure, removable only via a procedure analogous to state legislative impeachment.
2. **Revitalizing the Inter-State Council (Article 263):**
   - Transform it from an ad-hoc deliberative forum into a decisive inter-governmental decision-making institution.
   - Mandate that all major national bills dealing with Concurrent List subjects must be formally discussed in the ISC before parliamentary introduction.
3. **Fiscal Federalism & Rationalisation of Cesses:**
   - Place a constitutional ceiling on the percentage of cesses and surcharges that the Union can levy under Article 271, or include all cesses within the divisible pool of taxes shared with the states.
   - Expand the representation of States in the formulation of Terms of Reference (ToR) for the Finance Commission.
4. **Institutionalization of the GST Council (Article 279A):**
   - Enhance the voting weight of state governments and institute an independent GST Dispute Settlement Mechanism to resolve revenue-sharing deadlocks.
5. **Modernizing the 7th Schedule:**
   - Review and update the Union, State, and Concurrent Lists to incorporate 21st-century realities: cyber security, artificial intelligence, climate adaptation, cross-border digital taxation, and public health federalism.

#### Question 13: NITI Aayog vs. Planning Commission
> *"Write / short note on Niti Aayog"*

| Feature | Erstwhile Planning Commission (1950–2014) | NITI Aayog (National Institution for Transforming India) (2015–Present) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Origin & Nature** | Extra-constitutional body established by executive resolution of Nehru cabinet (1950). | Extra-constitutional think-tank created by Union Cabinet resolution on January 1, 2015. |
| **Planning Paradigm** | **Top-Down ("One-Size-Fits-All"):** Centralized five-year plans imposed uniformly on states. | **Bottom-Up ("Team India"):** Recognizes local diversity; plans formulated from village/state levels. |
| **Financial Powers** | Allocated discretionary grants and developmental funds to states under Article 282. | **Zero Financial Allocation Powers:** Purely advisory and knowledge-generating think-tank. |
| **State Representation** | States were passive recipients represented only in the infrequent National Development Council (NDC). | **Governing Council:** Chief Ministers and Lieutenant Governors are permanent members, meeting regularly. |
| **Core Philosophy** | Command economy, socialist developmentalism, state allocation of capital. | Competitive-Cooperative Federalism, private innovation, ranking states via performance indices. |

- **Critical Appraisal:** While NITI Aayog succeeded in instilling competitive efficiency through data-driven indices, critics (such as Prabhat Patnaik and Balveer Arora) observe that the loss of financial allocation powers has left poorer states without an institutional champion to compensate for regional developmental deficits.

#### Question 14: Role of the Finance Commission & Fiscal Federalism
> *"Write short note on Finance commission."*

##### 1. Constitutional Mandate (Article 280)
- Quasi-judicial body constituted by the President of India every fifth year (or earlier).
- Acts as the **balancing wheel of Indian fiscal federalism**, tasked with making recommendations regarding:
  1. **Vertical Devolution:** The distribution of the net proceeds of taxes between the Union and the States.
  2. **Horizontal Allocation:** The allocation between the States of their respective shares of such proceeds.
  3. **Grants-in-Aid (Article 275):** The principles governing grants-in-aid to states in need of revenue deficit assistance.
  4. **Panchayat and Municipal Finances (Articles 280(3)(bb) and (c)):** Measures to augment state consolidated funds to supplement local bodies.

##### 2. Key Evolutionary Benchmarks
- **14th Finance Commission (Y.V. Reddy):** Executed a historic paradigm shift by increasing the states' share in the net divisible pool of central taxes from **32% to 42%**, drastically expanding untied, unconditional fiscal transfers to state governments.
- **15th Finance Commission (N.K. Singh):**
  - Maintained devolution at **41%** (adjusting 1% for the newly created Union Territories of Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh).
  - Adopted an innovative criteria matrix: Population (2011 Census - 15%), Income Distance (45%), Area (15%), Forest & Ecology (10%), Demographic Performance (12.5%), and Tax Effort (2.5%).
  - Incentivized states for population stabilization via the "Demographic Performance" criterion to address the historical grievances of Southern states who argued they were penalized for superior family planning and socio-economic progress.
