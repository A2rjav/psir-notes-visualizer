## 1. Biographical Hermeneutics & The Subaltern Organic Intellectual

Dr. Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar (14 April 1891 – 6 December 1956) occupies an unmatched, radical position in modern Indian political philosophy. As his biographer **Dhananjay Keer** famously observed:
> *"Ambedkar is the tallest leader among Dalits. No one before Ambedkar or after Ambedkar could achieve what he could do for members of his community."*

### A. Lived Experience and the Epistemology of Oppression
Unlike bourgeois and upper-caste nationalists whose political philosophies were distilled from philosophical contemplation, British liberal jurisprudence, or Vedantic revivalism, Ambedkar's political philosophy was rooted in the direct, visceral trauma of untouchability (*Asprishyata*):
1. **The Mahar Community Heritage:** Born into the Mahar caste in Mhow (Central Provinces), Ambedkar experienced systematic segregation despite his father's service in the British Indian Army.
2. **Western Academic Attainment:** Through scholarships provided by the Maharaja of Baroda (Sayajirao Gaekwad III) and the Maharaja of Kolhapur (Chhatrapati Shahu Maharaj), Ambedkar attained elite doctoral and legal qualifications from Columbia University (under John Dewey, Edwin Seligman, and Alexander Goldenweiser) and the London School of Economics / Gray's Inn.
3. **The Professional Reality of Caste in Bombay:** Upon returning as an impeccably credentialed barrister, he found that high-caste Hindu litigants and solicitors refused his services because his touch was deemed polluting. This personal encounter proved that class elevation, western education, and professional distinction could not overcome the ontological stigma of caste. As Ambedkar realized:
   $$\text{Modern Education} + \text{Economic Advancement} \neq \text{Social Dignity under Brahmanical Hegemony}$$

### B. Institutionalization of Dalit Agency: Journalistic and Political Vehicles
Ambedkar recognized that subaltern emancipation required self-representation, counter-publics, and institutional autonomy, discarding upper-caste patron-client benevolence:
- **Journalistic Interventions:**
  - *Mooknayak* (1920; "Leader of the Dumb"): Gave voice to the silenced untouchables.
  - *Bahishkrit Bharat* (1927; "Excluded India"): Advocated radical civil rights against ritual orthodoxy.
  - *Janata* (1930) and *Prabuddha Bharat* (1956).
- **Socio-Educational Platforms:**
  - *Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha* (1924; Central slogan: *"Educate, Agitate, Organise"*).
- **Direct Action for Civil Rights:**
  - *Mahad Satyagraha* (20 March 1927): Asserting the civil right of untouchables to drink water from the Chhadar Tank. Ambedkar called this an ontological assertion of human equality, comparing it to the French Revolution's Fall of the Bastille. When Gandhi declined to endorse Mahad as a national satyagraha on the ground that satyagraha should be reserved against foreign rule, Ambedkar realized the profound limits of Congress nationalism.
  - *Manusmriti Dahan* (25 December 1927): The public burning of the *Manusmriti* as the foundational ideological text of graded inequality.
  - *Kalaram Temple Entry Satyagraha* (Nasik, 1930–1935): Demonstrating that Hindu orthodoxy would never voluntarily open religious sanctuaries to Dalits.
- **Independent Political Formations:**
  - *Independent Labour Party* (1936): A class-caste broad coalition uniting workers, peasants, and Depressed Classes.
  - *Scheduled Castes Federation* (1942): Focused on constitutional safeguards and separate political identity.
  - *Republican Party of India* (conceived 1956, formed 1957): Aimed at building a secular, rights-based socialist democracy.

## 2. The Nationalist Dilemma: Arun Shourie vs Christophe Jaffrelot

Ambedkar remains one of the most vigorously debated figures in modern Indian historiography due to his stance on the Indian National Congress, the Quit India Movement, and British imperialism.

### A. Arun Shourie's Critique: *Worshipping False Gods* (1997)
Conservative journalist and politician Arun Shourie leveled a fierce polemic against Ambedkar, alleging that he was a British collaborator who sabotaged India's freedom struggle:
1. **Opposition to Purna Swaraj (1929):** Ambedkar refused to endorse the Congress declaration of complete independence at Lahore, arguing that political independence without social democratization would merely substitute a British bureaucracy with a high-caste Hindu oligarchy.
2. **Nagpur Address (8 August 1930):** In his presidential address to the All India Depressed Classes Congress at Nagpur, Ambedkar explicitly stated that the depressed classes could not support an unconditional project of national independence that failed to guarantee their fundamental citizenship rights.
3. **The 1939 Prioritisation Statement:** In 1939, Ambedkar stated unequivocally:
   > *"Whenever there is a conflict of interest between the country and the Untouchables, I will give preference to the Untouchables."*
4. **Opposition to Quit India (1942):** Ambedkar condemned the 1942 Quit India Movement as irresponsible, adventurist, and detrimental to the war against global fascism.
5. **Colonial Executive Collaboration:** Ambedkar accepted appointment as Labour Member in the **Viceroy's Executive Council (1942–1946)** and served on the **National Defence Council (Defence Advisory Committee)**. Shourie argues these bodies were set up by Britain merely to gain legitimacy for mobilizing Indian resources for World War II without conceding independence.
6. **Support for Partition:** Ambedkar's treatise *Thoughts on Pakistan* (1941) supported Jinnah's right to self-determination, further fueling Shourie's accusations of an anti-national posture.

### B. Christophe Jaffrelot's Defense: Subaltern Majority and the "Bahujan" Nation
Political scientist **Christophe Jaffrelot** (*Dr. Ambedkar and Untouchability: Analyzing and Fighting Caste*, 2005) provides a robust academic defense, showing that labeling Ambedkar "anti-national" is an absurdity born of upper-caste nationalist hegemony:
1. **The Demographic Majority of the *Bahujan Samaj*:** Ambedkar represented the Dalits (Depressed Classes), Shudras (Other Backward Classes), Adivasis (Tribals), religious minorities, and impoverished workers. Together, these groups constituted over 75–80% of India's population. Jaffrelot argues:
   $$\text{A leader championing the human rights of 80\% of a society can never be termed "anti-national".}$$
2. **Nationalism as an Elite Class-Caste Weapon:** Ambedkar understood that the Indian National Congress was dominated by urban high-caste elites (Brahmins, Kayasthas, Banias). For these elites, "nationalism" operated as an ideological smoke screen to suppress internal class and caste conflicts and delegitimise subaltern demands for equality as "divisive" or "pro-British."
3. **Tactical Use of the Colonial State:** Following Jyotiba Phule, Ambedkar recognized that British colonial jurisprudence—though exploitative in political economy—introduced the principle of rule of law, legal equality, and universal education. For Dalits, the British Raj was an external counterweight against the millennium-long domestic tyranny of orthodox Brahminism.

## 3. The Anti-Caste Tradition: Jyotiba Phule's Legacy

Ambedkar openly acknowledged three spiritual and intellectual gurus: **Gautama Buddha**, **Kabir**, and **Mahatma Jyotirao Phule (1827–1890)**.

### A. British Raj vs. Peshwa Raj
Under the orthodox Chitpavan Brahmin **Peshwa Raj** (18th century Maharashtra), the persecution of Mahars reached horrifying extremes:
- Untouchables were forced to tie a black thread around their wrists or necks.
- They had to hang an earthen pot around their necks to catch their spit, lest their saliva defile the earth.
- They had to tie a broom behind their waists to sweep away their footprints as they walked, preventing upper-caste Hindus from stepping on their impure traces.
- Entry into the city of Poona was strictly forbidden between 9:00 AM and 3:00 PM because that was when shadows were longest, creating the risk that an untouchable's shadow might pollute a Brahmin.

When the British East India Company's forces (composed predominantly of Mahar soldiers) defeated the Peshwa's army at the **Battle of Koregaon (1 January 1818)**, it was perceived in the subaltern consciousness not as British conquest, but as an act of Dalit liberation from Peshwa tyranny. Ambedkar commemorated this battle annually at the Koregaon Vijay Stambha.

Phule argued in *Gulamgiri* (1873) that British rule was a divine dispensation that broke the monopoly of Brahminical knowledge, brought modern schooling, and instituted legal rights. Ambedkar fully embraced this insight:
> *"The British delivered us from the long age of tyranny by Orthodox Hindus."*

## 4. Ambedkar on Nation and Nationalism

Ambedkar developed an incisive critique of the bourgeois-nationalist narrative that posited India as an ancient, organic nation.

```

• No shared social consciousness (*We-feeling*)        Equality, and Fraternity
• Nationalism used by elites to muzzle subalterns    • Consummation in 1946 Constituent Address
```

### A. Deconstruction of the Myth of an Indian Nation
1. **Absence of Organic Fraternity:** Drawing on French philosopher **Ernest Renan**'s definition of a nation as a "spiritual principle" and a "daily plebiscite" rooted in shared memories and solidarity, Ambedkar argued that caste society is inherently anti-national.
2. **A Society of Warring Camps:** India was not a nation, but a conglomeration of isolated, self-enclosed castes living in states of perpetual mutual antipathy and graded jealousy. Caste destroys public spirit, mutual sympathy, and common purpose.
3. **Nationalism as Hegemonic Rhetoric:** In the hands of Congress leaders, "nationalism" functioned as an ideological weapon to dismiss subaltern social grievances. Demands for separate electorates or reservation were painted as "treason against the Motherland."

### B. The Ideal of a True Nation: Address to the Constituent Assembly (December 1946)
Ambedkar did not reject the ideal of an Indian nation; he rejected the false claim that India *already was* one. He insisted that the first step toward nationhood was candid self-diagnosis:
> *"I know today we are divided politically, economically and socially. We are a group of warring camps and I am probably a leader of each one of such camps. But with all our differences, I am convinced that a day will come when we will forget these differences and become one nation."*
> — *Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Speech in Constituent Assembly, 17 December 1946*

In his closing speech to the Constituent Assembly (25 November 1949), Ambedkar reiterated this foundational warning:
- Political democracy cannot survive without underlying **social democracy**.
- Social democracy rests on the inseparable trinity of **Liberty, Equality, and Fraternity**:
  $$\text{Social Democracy} = \text{Liberty} + \text{Equality} + \text{Fraternity}$$
- Without fraternity, liberty produces the supremacy of the few over the many, and equality suppresses individual initiative.

## 5. Anthropological Theory of Caste and Untouchability

Ambedkar rejected religious myths, mystical cosmologies, and racial theories of caste, advancing instead a rigorous historical-anthropological framework across his major works.

```
                     GENEALOGY OF CASTE & UNTOUCHABILITY
                                      │
       ┌──────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────┐
       ▼                                                             ▼
*WHO WERE THE SHUDRAS?* (1946)                        *THE UNTOUCHABLES* (1948)
───────────────────────────────                        ──────────────────────────
• Original 3 Varnas (Rigvedic period)                  • Untouchables are NOT Shudras
• Shudras were Solar Kshatriyas                        • Outside the Varna System (*Avarnas*)
• Conflict with Brahmins (Paijavana vs Vasistha)       • Origin: "Broken Men" of defeated tribes
• Deprivation of *Upanayana Sanskar* (Purification)    • Settled Agriculture vs Nomadic Raiders
• Fall into fourth degraded status                     • Broken Men hired as village guards (outside)
                                                       • Converts to Buddhism
                                                       • Retaliation: Beef-eating taboo vs Vegetarianism
```

### A. Rejection of the Rigvedic *Purushasukta*
Orthodox Hinduism derives the fourfold Varna system from Rigveda (Mandala X, Hymn 90), which claims:
- Brahmins emerged from the mouth of the Cosmic Being (*Purusha*).
- Kshatriyas from his arms.
- Vaishyas from his thighs.
- Shudras from his feet.

Ambedkar denounced this hymn as a late, fabricated interpolation designed to provide divine sanction to an exploitative social order. He argued that human anatomy cannot determine social status, and sought a rational, historical explanation.

### B. Transition to Settled Life & The Origin of the Shudras
In *Who Were the Shudras? How They Came to Be the Fourth Varna in Indo-Aryan Society* (1946):
1. **Three Original Varnas:** Indo-Aryan society initially had only three varnas: Brahmins, Kshatriyas, and Vaishyas.
2. **Shudras as Solar Kshatriyas:** Shudras were originally part of the Kshatriya varna in the solar race (*Suryavanshi*), holding prominent kingly offices (e.g., King Sudas in the Rigveda).
3. **The Sacerdotal Conflict:** A protracted conflict broke out between Kshatriya rulers and Brahmin priests (symbolized by the rivalry between Vishvamitra and Vasistha).
4. **Denial of *Upanayana Sanskar*:** Brahmins retaliated against hostile Kshatriya clans by refusing to perform their *Upanayana Sanskar* (sacred thread investiture ceremony). In Indo-Aryan society, *Upanayana* was the essential sacrament of social purification and twice-born (*Dvija*) rebirth. Stripped of *Upanayana*, these Kshatriyas were reduced to ritual impurity and degraded into the fourth status: the Shudras.

### C. The Origin of Untouchability: The "Broken Men" Theory
In *The Untouchables: Who Were They and Why They Became Untouchables?* (1948), Ambedkar distinguished between Shudras (within the Varna system) and Untouchables (entirely outside the Varna system: *Avarnas*, *Atishudras*, *Chandals*, *Antyajas*):
1. **Nomadic vs Settled Society:** The transition from nomadic pastoralism to settled agriculture caused tribal warfare. Victorious tribes settled in agricultural villages; defeated tribes were shattered and scattered into wandering fragments termed **"Broken Men"**.
2. **The Security Contract:** Settled villagers needed protection from nomadic bandits; the Broken Men needed food and shelter. The villagers allowed the Broken Men to live outside the village boundaries (*Bahya*) on the condition that they perform watch-and-ward duties and manual service.
3. **Buddhism as the Religion of the Broken Men:** When the Buddha challenged Vedic ritualism, animal sacrifice, and caste supremacy, the Broken Men embraced Buddhism en masse.
4. **Brahmanical Counter-Revolution:** When Brahminism re-established political supremacy during the Gupta period (under the *Manusmriti*), Buddhists who refused to surrender to Brahminical hegemony were systematically segregated, demonized as *Mlechhas* and *Chandals*, and subjected to permanent ritual pollution.

### D. The Dietary Weapon: Beef-Eating and Vegetarianism
Ambedkar overturned the orthodox claim that untouchables were unclean because of beef-eating:
- In the Vedic era, Brahmins were enthusiastic meat and beef-eaters (as evidenced in ancient texts like the *Satapatha Brahmana*).
- When Buddhism gained mass popularity through its doctrine of *Ahimsa* (non-injury to animals), Brahmins felt threatened.
- To regain supremacy over Buddhism, Brahmins executed a radical reversal: they renounced beef, embraced strict vegetarianism, and sacralized the cow.
- The Broken Men, impoverished and living on the margins, could not afford to abandon eating the carcasses of dead cows (*carrion*). Brahmins used this dietary habit to stigmatize them as permanently impure and untouchable.

## 6. Mechanisms of Caste Domination: Endogamy, Graded Inequality, and Sanskritisation

Ambedkar identified two core sociological mechanisms that sustain the caste order: **Endogamy** and the principle of **Purity and Pollution**.

```
                         THE ARCHITECTURE OF CASTE
                                     │
        ┌────────────────────────────┴────────────────────────────┐
        ▼                                                         ▼
ENDOGAMY (*Sva-jati Vivaha*)                         PURITY AND POLLUTION
─────────────────────────────                         ────────────────────
• Artificial enclosure of exogamous clans             • Graded Inequality (*Sopana-vyavastha*)
• Imposed by Brahmins to preserve "purity"            • Ascending reverence & descending contempt
• Copied by other castes (Sanskritisation)           • Dietary segregation (Beef-eating vs Vegetarianism)
• Prevents fraternity and social inter-mixing         • Irreparable division of labourers
```

### A. The Creation of Caste through Endogamy
In his foundational 1916 paper at Columbia University, *Castes in India: Their Mechanism, Genesis and Development*, Ambedkar showed:
1. **Primacy of Exogamy in Tribal India:** Ancient Indian tribes were exogamous (marrying outside the *Gotra* or clan).
2. **The Brahminical Enclosure:** Brahmins enclosed their group by imposing strict endogamy (marrying only within the sub-caste) and forbidding exogamy to monopolize religious and social privileges.
3. **Imitation by Subordinate Castes:** Other classes, observing the supreme prestige of Brahmins, closed their own boundaries through imitation. Thus, castes are **"enclosed classes"**.
4. **Deconstruction of the "Pure Blood" Myth:** Ambedkar proved that no caste in India possesses biologically pure blood. Extensive racial and genetic miscegenation had already occurred long before endogamous enclosures were erected. Endogamy does not preserve racial purity; it preserves institutionalized social inequality.

### B. M.N. Srinivas and Sanskritisation
Ambedkar's analysis anticipated the concept of **Sanskritisation** formulated by sociologist **M.N. Srinivas** (*Religion and Society among the Coorgs of South India*, 1952):
- **Definition:** The process by which low castes adopt the customs, rituals, vegetarian diet, and life-ways of upper-caste "twice-born" Hindus to claim higher status.
- **Ambedkar's Critique of Sanskritisation:** Ambedkar pointed out that Sanskritisation does not destroy caste; it reinforces the legitimacy of the Brahminical hierarchy by making lower castes aspire to emulate their oppressors rather than annihilate the system.

### C. Division of Labour vs Division of Labourers
In *Annihilation of Caste* (1936), Ambedkar dismantled the conservative defense that caste is merely a traditional division of labour:
> *"The caste system is not merely a division of labour. It is also a division of labourers."*
1. **Involuntary and Hereditary:** It ties individuals to professions based on the accident of birth, regardless of natural aptitude or choice.
2. **Hierarchical Stratification:** Unlike ordinary divisions of labour in industrial societies, it arranges labourers in a rigid, vertical hierarchy of **graded inequality**—where every caste looks down upon the one below it and resents the one above it.
3. **Prevention of Solidarity:** Graded inequality prevents the formation of common class consciousness, because even intermediate oppressed castes derive psychological gratification from having castes below them to exploit.

## 7. Annihilation of Caste (1936) & The Radical Exit from Hinduism

Ambedkar concluded that caste cannot be reformed from within Hinduism; it must be destroyed at its ideological root.

```
                           THE STRATEGY OF EMANCIPATION
                                         │
        ┌────────────────────────────────┴────────────────────────────────┐
        ▼                                                                 ▼
CRITIQUE OF HINDUISM & REFORMISM                                THE BUDDHIST EXIT (NAVAYANA)
────────────────────────────────                                ────────────────────────────
• Inequality sanctified in philosophy (*Shastras*)              • Conversion at Nagpur: 14 Oct 1956
• Bhakti & Arya Samaj failed structurally                      • "I will not die a Hindu"
• "Put dynamite on Vedas and Manusmriti"                        • Religion of Liberty, Equality, Fraternity
• Gandhi's moralism cannot transform society                   • 22 Oaths repudiating Hindu deities
```

### A. The Structural Incurability of Hinduism
Ambedkar drew a sharp contrast between Hinduism and other world religions:
- In other societies, inequality exists in *practice*, but their religious philosophies profess spiritual equality (e.g., equality before God in Christianity and Islam).
- In Hinduism, **inequality exists in religious philosophy itself**. The sacred texts (*Shrutis* and *Smritis*) sanctify graded discrimination as cosmic order (*Dharma*).
- Ambedkar expressed this indignantly:
  > *"A religion which forces someone to remain poor, which forces the illiterate to remain illiterate, which allows human beings to touch cow dung but not to touch a fellow human being, is not a religion but madness."*
- He characterized orthodox Hindu society as a **"race of losers"**—a collection of mutually hostile, warring castes unable to unite in defense of common civic values.

### B. The Futility of Internal Reform: Bhakti Saints and Mahatma Gandhi
1. **The Bhakti Movement:** Reformers like Kabir, Chokhamela, and Ravidas preached devotional equality, yet the caste system emerged from the medieval era intact, because Bhakti targeted personal devotion without destroying the authority of the Shastras.
2. **The Limitations of Gandhi:** Gandhi was a political leader, not a spiritual or religious reformer. While Hindus obeyed Gandhi's political calls for boycotts, they ignored his appeals to abolish untouchability in their personal lives.
3. **The Radical Prescription:**
   > *"You must give a new life to the Hindu society by dynamiting the Vedas and the Smritis, for they have sanctified caste."*

### C. The Existential Vow and the Buddhist Conversion (1956)
At the Yeola Conversion Conference (13 October 1935), Ambedkar took his historic vow:
> *"I was born a Hindu, because I had no choice in it; but I will not die a Hindu, for that is in my power."*

On **14 October 1956** (noted in handwritten notes as 14 November 1956), at Deekshabhoomi in Nagpur, Ambedkar, accompanied by over 500,000 followers, converted to Buddhism. He delivered 22 vows renouncing Hindu deities, rituals, and caste hierarchies. He defined this not as escapism, but as a moral and spiritual rebirth (**Navayana**)—a rational, egalitarian philosophy founded on compassion (*Karuna*), wisdom (*Prajna*), and equality (*Samata*).

## Model Answer Framework 1

### Question:
*"Caste is not merely a division of labour, but a division of labourers." Critically examine Dr. B.R. Ambedkar's critique of the caste system and explain why he considered its annihilation essential for Indian democracy.* (20 Marks / 250 Words)

### 1. Introduction: Contextualizing Ambedkar's Intervention
- Dr. B.R. Ambedkar's *Annihilation of Caste* (1936), originally prepared as an undelivered presidential address for the *Jat-Pat Todak Mandal* of Lahore, constitutes one of the most radical critiques of traditional Hindu social stratification.
- Ambedkar directly counters orthodox apologists and conservative functionalists who defend the caste (*Chaturvarnya*) system as a harmless, traditional system of division of labour essential for economic efficiency.

### 2. Core Theoretical Argument: Division of Labour vs Division of Labourers
Ambedkar proves that caste is fundamentally distinct from an ordinary economic division of labour:
1. **Involuntary and Hereditary Predetermination:** Unlike modern divisions of labour based on aptitude, training, and free choice, caste predetermines occupations before birth based on parental caste, causing what Ambedkar termed *"a monster of social injustice"* that stunts individual capacity.
2. **Graded Inequality (*Sopana-vyavastha*):** As **Prof. Sukhadeo Thorat** notes, caste arranges occupations in an immutable hierarchy of purity and pollution. Workers are not merely divided; they are graded one above the other, generating ascending reverence and descending contempt.
3. **Disassociation of Work from Dignity:** By branding manual labour as ritually polluting and reserving intellectual labour for the twice-born, caste creates economic inefficiency, social stigma, and forced occupational immobility.
4. **Prevention of Subaltern Solidarity:** Because inequality is graded rather than binary, each caste exercises dominion over the caste below it. This fractures the exploited masses into "warring camps," preventing unified proletarian resistance against high-caste hegemony.

### 3. Sociological Mechanisms Sustaining Caste: Endogamy and Sanskritisation
- **Endogamy as Enclosure:** In his 1916 paper *Castes in India*, Ambedkar demonstrated that caste was created by Brahmins closing their marriage boundaries through endogamy, forcing other classes to imitate this enclosure.
- **Limits of Sanskritisation:** As **M.N. Srinivas** later observed, lower castes attempt upward mobility by emulating upper-caste rituals (*Sanskritisation*). However, Ambedkar argued that Sanskritisation merely legitimized the Brahminical standard, proving that caste cannot be reformed by imitation, but only by structural destruction.

### 4. Why Annihilation of Caste is Essential for Democracy
- **Social Democracy as the Foundation of Political Democracy:** In his address to the Constituent Assembly on 25 November 1949, Ambedkar warned that India was entering a life of contradictions: *political equality* (one person, one vote) resting upon *social and economic inequality* (one person, one value).
- **The Principle of Associated Living:** Drawing on his teacher **John Dewey**, Ambedkar defined democracy not merely as a form of government, but as *"a mode of associated living, of conjoint communicated experience."* Caste destroys communication, empathy, and fraternity (*Maitri*). Without fraternity, liberty and equality cannot take root.

### 5. Conclusion
- As **Gail Omvedt** (*Dalits and the Democratic Revolution*) summarizes, Ambedkar transformed the Dalit struggle from a petition for charity into an uncompromising battle for structural citizenship and constitutional morality.
- Therefore, the annihilation of caste is not merely an internal Hindu reform, but the foundational prerequisite for realizing India as an authentic, egalitarian Republic.

## Model Answer Framework 2

### Question:
*Critically evaluate the contrasting perspectives of Arun Shourie and Christophe Jaffrelot regarding Dr. B.R. Ambedkar's nationalist credentials.* (15 Marks / 150 Words)

### 1. Introduction: The Contested Historiography of Ambedkar's Nationalism
- While mainstream nationalist historiography traditionally framed India's freedom struggle through an Indian National Congress-centric lens, subaltern and critical scholars have emphasized alternative, competing imaginations of the nation.
- The debate between **Arun Shourie** (*Worshipping False Gods*, 1997) and **Christophe Jaffrelot** (*Dr. Ambedkar and Untouchability*, 2005) highlights this deep ideological divide.

### 2. Arun Shourie's Critique: The Charge of Colonial Collaboration
Shourie argues that Ambedkar stood outside and opposed the mainstream freedom movement:
- **Opposition to Key Mobilizations:** Ambedkar opposed the 1929 Purna Swaraj declaration, distanced Dalits from the Civil Disobedience Movement, and condemned the 1942 Quit India Movement.
- **Institutional Alignment with Colonial Rule:** Serving on the Defence Advisory Committee (1941) and the Viceroy's Executive Council (1942–46) as Labour Member, which Shourie contends provided legitimacy to the British war effort.
- **Sectional Priority:** His 1939 declaration that between the interests of the country and the Untouchables, he would prioritize the Untouchables.

### 3. Christophe Jaffrelot's Defense: Subaltern Representation and Democratization
Jaffrelot refutes Shourie by contextualizing Ambedkar's strategy within sociological and democratic theory:
- **Representation of the Demographic Majority:** Ambedkar represented the *Bahujan Samaj* (Untouchables, Shudras, Tribals, minorities), who constituted the overwhelming majority of India. A leader advocating for the fundamental rights of 80% of a society cannot be termed anti-national.
- **Nationalism as Hegemonic Ideology:** Following **Jyotiba Phule**, Ambedkar realized that Congress-led nationalism was weaponized by high-caste elites to delegitimise subaltern demands for equality and justice.
- **Democratization Before Independence:** For Ambedkar, freedom from British rule without constitutional guarantees for Dalits would merely substitute British bureaucratic rule with Brahminical social tyranny.

### 4. Conclusion
- As **Valerian Rodrigues** points out, Ambedkar was not "anti-national"; he was an architect of a **"Nation-in-the-making."**
- Ambedkar's December 1946 Constituent Assembly address proved his ultimate vision: building a genuine nation founded on liberty, equality, and fraternity rather than papering over oppressive social divisions.
