## Thematic Overview & Architectural Roadmap

This batch of lecture notes covers the core constitutional, institutional, and political dynamics of Indian federalism, focusing on:
1. **Inter-State River Water Disputes (Pages 191–196):**
   - Constitutional division: State List Entry 17 vs. Union List Entry 56.
   - Article 262 and the Inter-State River Water Disputes (IRWD) Act, 1956.
   - Exclusion of Supreme Court jurisdiction under Article 262 vs. Judicial review via Special Leave Petitions (Article 136).
   - Institutional mechanisms: River Boards Act (1956), Inter-State Council (Article 263), Zonal Councils.
   - The Tribunal system: Procedural bottlenecks, 2002 amendments, proposed permanent tribunals, Dispute Resolution Committees (DRC).
   - Case studies: Cauvery Water Dispute, Sutlej-Yamuna Link (SYL) Canal, Narmada, and Krishna disputes.
   - Scholarly debate: Legal adjudication (*H.M. Seervai*) vs. Negotiated political settlement (*Ramaswamy R. Iyer*).
2. **Inter-State Boundary & Economic Disputes (Page 197):**
   - Territorial disputes: Belgaum (Maharashtra vs. Karnataka), Abohar-Fazilka (Punjab vs. Haryana), Chandigarh.
   - Economic/regional divides: Developed vs. BIMARU states; Producer/manufacturing vs. Consumer states; Nativism and anti-migrant politics.
   - Constitutional doctrines: Pith and Substance, Colourable Legislation.
3. **Centre-State Legislative Relations (Pages 197–199):**
   - Scheme of 7th Schedule: Union List (97 → 100/101), State List (66 → 61), Concurrent List (47 → 52).
   - Residuary Powers (Article 248) vested in the Centre.
   - Extraordinary Union legislative powers over State List: Article 249 (Rajya Sabha resolution), Article 250 (National Emergency), Article 252 (Two or more states request — "permanent transfer"), Article 253 (Treaty implementation without state consultation).
   - Reservation of State Bills for Presidential consideration (Articles 200 & 201) and pocket veto; Punchhi Commission’s 6-month timeline.
4. **Centre-State Administrative Relations (Pages 199–201):**
   - Conflictual dimensions: Union directions (Articles 256, 257) backed by coercive sanction under Article 365.
   - All India Services (Articles 312, 315–323) as instruments of Union oversight vs. State autonomy; Civil Services Boards.
   - Deployment of Union Armed/Paramilitary Forces (CRPF, BSF) in states without state consent.
5. **Article 356 (President's Rule) & Constitutional Pathology (Pages 199–201):**
   - Colonial roots in Section 93 of the Government of India Act, 1935.
   - Dr. B.R. Ambedkar’s defense: Intended as a "dead letter" precaution; subsequent misuse over 100 times.
   - Partisan abuse: 1977 (Janata Party) and 1980 (Congress) mass dissolutions; link to sub-national alienation and militancy (Punjab, J&K).
   - Landmark jurisprudence: *S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)* — Federalism and secularism as Basic Structure; mandatory floor tests; judicial scrutiny of material; suspended animation; remedy of restoration (exemplified in Uttarakhand and Arunachal Pradesh, 2016).
6. **The Institution of Governor (Page 202):**
   - Dual constitutional role: Constitutional head of State Executive vs. Agent/representative of the Union ("Wearing two hats on a single head").
   - Sarkaria Commission: Governor as the *"lynchpin of cooperative federalism"*.
   - Paul Brass’s perspective: Indian Constitution as a *"product of fear and insecurity"*; Governor as the *"eyes and ears of the Union"*.

### [Heading / Core Question]
**Q. Discuss the constitutional provisions with respect to resolution of water disputes.**

---

### 1. Constitutional Allocation of Powers (7th Schedule)

1. **State List (List II), Entry 17:**
   - Deals with the use and consumption of water.
   - Covers: Creation of system of irrigation, canals, drainage, embankments, water storage, dams, and water power, *subject to the provisions of Entry 56 of List I*.
2. **Union List (List I), Entry 56:**
   - Vests power in the Union w.r.t. regulation and development of inter-state rivers and river valleys to the extent to which such regulation and development under the control of the Union is declared by Parliament by law to be expedient in the public interest.

### 2. Nature of River Water Resources
- Most of the major rivers in India are **inter-state rivers**.
- Rivers are **mobile resources**, unlike land, forests, and minerals which are **static**.
- Because water is a flowing and fugitive resource, actions taken upstream (damming, diversion, extraction) directly impact riparian rights downstream, generating inherent inter-jurisdictional conflict.

### 3. Critical Analysis of the Role of the Union
- The **Union has not played its expected role**:
  - The Union government has preferred to treat water as if it were entirely a State subject.
  - The Union has vast constitutional scope under Entry 56 to regulate inter-state rivers and river valleys in the *national interest*, but has historically shied away from exercising comprehensive basin-wide statutory leadership.
  - The purpose of placing Entry 56 in the Union List was to ensure that river basin management is approached from a **national, holistic perspective** rather than through parochial state lenses.
  - In practice, the Union government has engaged in **political balancing of competing interests** of states and political parties, rather than treating water as a **strategic, vital national resource**.
  - **Comparative Global Best Practice:** In Australia, the federal/Commonwealth government proactively adopted and unified inter-state river management (e.g., the *Murray-Darling Basin Authority*).

---

### 4. Constitutional Mechanism to Deal with Water Disputes

> **Unique Constitutional Status:**
> Water is the **only inter-state dispute** for which there is a **special, dedicated provision** in the Constitution of India (Article 262).

#### Significance of Water
- **Mark Twain's Famous Dictum:**
  $$\text{"Whisky is to drink and water is to fight over."}$$
- It is widely predicted by geopolitical and environmental scholars that **future wars will be water wars** (both internationally and inter-state within federations).

### 1. The Pre-1956 Framework: Supreme Court under Article 131
- **Article 131 (Original Jurisdiction):**
  - The Supreme Court of India is a **federal court**, and its primary constitutional role is the peaceful adjudication and resolution of disputes between the federation and its units or between the units *inter se*.
  - Until 1956, the Supreme Court under Article 131 was the main institutional body for the resolution of inter-state water disputes.

### 2. The Legislative Watershed of 1956
In 1956, exercising powers under Article 262 and Entry 56 of List I, Parliament passed two landmark acts:
1. **Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 (IRWD Act)**
2. **River Boards Act, 1956**

---

### 3. Constitutional Basis and Ouster of Court Jurisdiction

#### Article 262 Provisions:
1. **Article 262(1):** Parliament may by law provide for the adjudication of any dispute or complaint with respect to the use, distribution, or control of the waters of, or in, any inter-state river or river valley.
2. **Article 262(2):** Parliament may by law provide that **neither the Supreme Court nor any other court shall exercise jurisdiction** in respect of any such dispute or complaint.

#### The Reality of Judicial Intervention:
- Under Section 11 of the IRWD Act, 1956, Parliament explicitly barred the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and other courts over water disputes referred to a tribunal.
- **However, the Supreme Court still entertains petitions under Special Leave Petitions (Article 136).**
- *Critique:* Ideally, the Supreme Court should discourage states from litigating river water disputes via SLPs and uphold parliamentary intent.
- **Comparative Example (US Supreme Court):**
  - In the USA, the only route available for inter-state disputes is the original jurisdiction of the US Supreme Court.
  - Even with original jurisdiction, the US Supreme Court strongly prefers to **direct states to pursue politically negotiated compacts/solutions** rather than forcing judicial decrees.
- **Fali S. Nariman’s View:**
  - Eminent jurist Fali S. Nariman has argued that **it is better to restore the pre-1956 system** (bringing disputes back under the Supreme Court's Article 131 jurisdiction).
  - *Rationale:* Immense time, public money, and administrative resources are wasted before tribunals, and at the end of the day, states still approach the Supreme Court via Article 136, which the Court inevitably entertains.

---

1. **H.M. Seervai (Constitutional Jurist):**
   - Held that it is a **misconception to believe that political settlements are more satisfactory** than judicial adjudication.
   - Political settlements in water disputes are vulnerable to electoral pressures, transient political coalitions, and lack of binding enforceability; formal legal adjudication provides constitutional finality.
2. **Ramaswamy R. Iyer (Eminent Water Policy Scholar):**
   - Argued that **formal judicial adjudication is an unnecessary and counterproductive method** of dealing with inter-state water disputes.
   - A **negotiated settlement is infinitely superior**.
   - *Why Adjudication Fails:*
     - Adjudication is inherently **adversarial and divisive**.
     - It encourages riparian states to make **maximalist, exaggerated, and rigid claims**.
     - It hardens public postures and turns water sharing into zero-sum identity politics.

### 5. Article 263: Inter-State Council
- Article 263 provides for the creation of an **Inter-State Council (ISC)** by Presidential Order to investigate, discuss, and deliberate on subjects of common interest between the Union and the States.
- The ISC would have been the **most preferred institutional mechanism** for resolving water disputes through consensus, given the ease with which it could be established (requiring merely an executive order of the President).
- Yet, despite strong recommendations from the **First Administrative Reforms Commission (ARC)** and the **Sarkaria Commission**, successive central governments delayed its creation... *(continued on Page 193)*

### 1. Inter-State Council & Zonal Councils: Institutional Neglect

- **Inter-State Council (Article 263):**
  - Prolonged delay by Union governments: Finally established only in **1990** under the **V.P. Singh Government** following the Sarkaria Commission report.
  - Even after its creation, this constitutional platform has **never been consistently or effectively utilised** for resolving inter-state water disputes.
- **Zonal Councils:**
  - Created under statutory authority by the **States Reorganisation Act, 1956**.
  - One of the primary agendas behind the creation of Zonal Councils was the **resolution of inter-state water disputes**.
  - *Historical Statement:* The then Union Home Minister (Govind Ballabh Pant) declared that *"water and rivers know no linguistic boundaries"*.
  - *Reality:* Uptil now, **not a single inter-state water dispute has been successfully handled or resolved** through the forum of Zonal Councils.

---

### 2. River Boards Act, 1956: A Dormant Statute
- Designed as a **proactive, cooperative mechanism** to make riparian states habitual of institutional cooperation.
- River boards were envisioned to be constituted for **joint planning, integrated basin management, conservation, and development** involving all states sharing a river basin under the leadership of the Union.
- *Tragic Outcome:* The River Boards Act, 1956 has remained virtually a **dead letter**; not a single river board has ever been constituted under this Act due to states guarding their territorial control and the Centre's political reluctance.

---

### 3. Critical Evaluation of the Tribunal System under IRWD Act, 1956

**Q. Critically evaluate the working of tribunal system w.r.t interstate water dispute.**

#### Salient Features of the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956:
1. **Narrow Definition of "Water Dispute":**
   - The Act specifies what can be treated as an inter-state water dispute under the Act.
   - Many varieties of inter-state river disputes are **excluded** from the tribunal's remit.
   - *Example:* The **Mullaperiyar Dam dispute** between Tamil Nadu and Kerala was treated as a safety/contract dispute, allowing parties to approach the Supreme Court directly under Article 131 rather than a water tribunal.
2. **Exclusion of Supreme Court Jurisdiction:**
   - Section 11 of the Act bars the Supreme Court and all other courts from exercising jurisdiction over disputes referred to a tribunal.

---

### 4. Systemic Pathologies of the Water Tribunal System

1. **Gatekeeping by the Union Government:**
   - Riparian states cannot approach the tribunal directly. The aggrieved party must approach the **Union Government**, which decides whether the dispute cannot be settled through negotiations.
2. **Inordinate Delay in Tribunal Constitution:**
   - *Original Flaw:* There was originally **no statutory time limit** within which the Union government was required to complete political negotiations and constitute a tribunal.
   - The Union government routinely took extraordinary time:
     - *Example:* It took **20 years** of continuous dispute and agitation to constitute the **Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal (CWDT)**, and that too only after mandatory directions from the Supreme Court!
   - *2002 Amendment Reform:* The Act was amended in 2002 to make it **mandatory for the Union to constitute a tribunal within 1 year** of receiving a request from a state.
3. **Protracted Delays in Delivering Awards:**
   - Initially, there was **no statutory time limit** for tribunals to deliver their final award.
   - *Example:* The **Cauvery Tribunal took 17 years** (1990 to 2007) to deliver its final award.

### 1. The 2002 Amendment: Timelines and Persistent Loopholes

- **Statutory Deadlines Introduced (2002):**
  - The tribunal must submit its report and award within **3 years**.
  - In exceptional situations, the Union government can extend the period by up to **2 additional years** (maximum total: 5 years).
- **The "Clarification Loophole":**
  - While the 2002 amendment plugged the loophole regarding the delivery of the initial award, it opened/left another critical loophole:
  - Once the award is submitted, the Union or State governments can seek **explanations or clarifications within 3 months**.
  - However, **there is NO statutory time limit** within which the tribunal must provide the clarification! This has led to endless post-award stagnation.

---

### 2. Institutional Gaps & Recent Reform Proposals

1. **Mandate for a Central Data Bank:**
   - The IRWD Act mandates that the Central Government maintain a comprehensive **national data bank and information system** for each river basin to facilitate objective fact-finding.
   - In practice, **no serious effort was made** for decades to build this neutral, transparent data bank.
   - *Recent Government Initiatives:* The Central Government has proposed a specialized body tasked with maintaining real-time empirical data on rainfall, water availability, flows, and storage.
2. **Single Permanent Tribunal with Benches:**
   - To eliminate delays associated with setting up ad-hoc tribunals for each dispute, the government introduced the *Inter-State River Water Disputes (Amendment) Bill* to constitute a **single, permanent Inter-State River Water Disputes Tribunal** with specialized benches.
3. **Dispute Resolution Committee (DRC):**
   - The proposed framework introduces a mandatory **Dispute Resolution Committee (DRC)** comprising technical experts and representatives to attempt an amicable, negotiated political settlement within 1 to 1.5 years before a matter is referred to the tribunal, avoiding unnecessary judicialization.

---

### 3. Composition of Water Tribunals

- **Present Statutory Structure:**
  - The Union Government requests the **Chief Justice of India (CJI)** to nominate judges.
  - The CJI constitutes a 3-member tribunal comprising sitting judges:
    - The Chairperson must be a sitting judge of the Supreme Court.
    - The other two members can be sitting judges of either the Supreme Court or High Courts.
  - **Assessors:** The tribunal is assisted by technical assessors (irrigation engineers, hydrologists) to guide judges in scientific assessment.
- **Proposed Permanent Tribunal Structure:**
  - Chaired by a retired Judge of the Supreme Court, with Vice-Chairpersons and judicial/expert members nominated to benches.
- **Punchhi Commission Recommendation on Composition:**
  - The **Punchhi Commission (2010)** recommended the introduction of a **multi-disciplinary tribunal**.
  - Rather than being dominated purely by legal judges, the tribunal should comprise diverse experts and representatives from:
    - Civil society
    - Social scientists
    - Environmentalists and ecological economists
    - Local self-government bodies (Panchayats and Municipalities)

---

### 4. Legal Status, Enforcement, and Interim Awards

- **Legal Power of the Award:**
  - The award/decision of the tribunal, once published, has the **same force as an order or decree of the Supreme Court**.
  - It is the constitutional duty of the Union Government to ensure the award is fully implemented.
  - The tribunal enjoys all powers of a **Civil Court** under the Code of Civil Procedure (CPC).
- **Interim Awards Controversy:**
  - Originally, the IRWD Act was silent on whether a tribunal possessed the power to grant interim relief.
  - *Supreme Court Clarification:* In the **Cauvery Water Dispute**, the Supreme Court held that the tribunal has the incidental and ancillary power to grant **interim awards** if urgent riparian necessity demands it.
  - *Enforceability Rule:* Whether it is an interim award or a final award, it becomes legally enforceable **only upon publication in the Official Gazette** by the Union Government.

### 1. Is a Water Tribunal Award Subject to Judicial Review?

- **Constitutional Position:**
  - The award itself is **not directly appealable** as an ordinary decree, and Article 262(2) expressly bars court jurisdiction over inter-state water disputes.
  - However, the Supreme Court has ruled that it **can entertain questions regarding the working, legality, and constitutional propriety of the tribunal**.

---

### 2. Constitutional Basis of Supreme Court Jurisdiction

- **Supervisory Jurisdiction:**
  - Under **Article 136** (Special Leave Petition), the Supreme Court exercises extraordinary supervisory jurisdiction over all courts and tribunals throughout the territory of India (with the sole constitutional exception of military tribunals).
- **The Landmark December 2016 Cauvery Ruling:**
  - In December 2016, in the *Cauvery Water Dispute matter*, the Supreme Court delivered a historic ruling affirming its jurisdiction:
    1. The Supreme Court held that it possesses **unfettered power under Article 136** to hear appeals against the final judgements and awards of river water tribunals.
    2. The Court clarified that the constitutional bar under Article 262 and Section 11 of the IRWD Act **only restricts the Court from entertaining original suits or complaints** (ousting Article 131 jurisdiction).
    3. The bar **DOES NOT strip the Supreme Court of its extraordinary appellate power** under Article 136 to hear appeals against tribunal awards.
  - *Consequence:* As a result of this landmark judgement, **all tribunal awards have been formally brought within the scope of Supreme Court judicial review**.

---

### 3. What is the Road Ahead?

1. **Legislative Amendments are Insufficient:**
   - Procedural amendments setting timelines are inadequate because there is **no credible mechanism to enforce ground-level implementation** when riparian states refuse to comply.
   - We must either:
     - **Restore the original jurisdiction of the Supreme Court** under Article 131 (bringing clarity and direct enforcement).
     - **Utilise inter-state institutional platforms** like the Zonal Councils or the Inter-State Council to arrive at politically negotiated, consensus-driven solutions.
2. **From Top-Down Technocracy to Participatory Ecological Governance:**
   - **Top-down, prescriptive, legalistic, bureaucratic solutions will not work** in emotive and vital resource sharing.
   - What is urgently required is a **participatory democratic process** involving all primary stakeholders (farmers, local communities, water-user associations, urban consumers).
   - Any lasting solution must address water requirements across multiple dimensions:
     - **Water for Life:** Drinking water, sanitation, and fundamental human rights.
     - **Water for Agriculture:** Food security and rural livelihoods.
     - **Water for Industry:** Sustainable economic productivity.
     - **Water for Aquatic Life:** Environmental flows, riverine ecology, and biodiversity conservation.
   - The solution must be **socially just, politically equitable, and environmentally sustainable**, safeguarding the interests of both the present and future generations (**intergenerational equity**).

---

### 4. Case Study from South India: The Cauvery River Dispute
- The **Cauvery water dispute** between Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Kerala, and Puducherry is the most illustrative case study in Indian federalism:
  - It led to critical **legal and constitutional clarifications** regarding the scope of the IRWD Act and the appellate powers of the Supreme Court.
  - Even though the statute expressly prohibits judicial intervention, the **Supreme Court was forced to intervene repeatedly** because the Union Government failed to fulfill its statutory obligations (e.g., constituting the tribunal, framing implementation schemes).
  - The dispute highlights:
    - Extraordinary delay in constitution (took 20 years).
    - Protracted delay in delivering the award (took 17 years).
    - Failure of implementation on the ground even **10 years after the final award was delivered in 2007**.

### 1. Case Study: The Sutlej-Yamuna Link (SYL) Canal Dispute

1. **Historical Genesis (1966):**
   - The dispute originated with the **Punjab Reorganisation Act, 1966**, which partitioned composite Punjab into Punjab and Haryana, creating Himachal Pradesh as a separate territory.
   - Haryana demanded its legitimate share of river waters agreed upon at the time of the creation of the state.
2. **Tribunal Award & Construction Commitment:**
   - The Eradi Water Tribunal was constituted and gave its award.
   - Haryana completed the construction of the SYL canal within its territory and paid advance funds to the State of Punjab to construct the canal link on the Punjab side.
3. **The 2004 Crisis — Unilateral Legislative Action:**
   - In 2004, the Punjab Legislative Assembly took an **unprecedented unilateral step** by enacting the *Punjab Termination of Agreements Act, 2004*, unilaterally abrogating all its water-sharing agreements with neighboring states.
   - The President of India referred the constitutional validity of this Act to the Supreme Court under its **advisory jurisdiction (Article 143)**.
4. **Supreme Court Judgement (2016):**
   - The Supreme Court held that the **Punjab Termination of Agreements Act, 2004 was unconstitutional and null and void**.
   - *Constitutional Principle:* A state legislature cannot unilaterally nullify an inter-state agreement or an order/decree of the Supreme Court; doing so violates the federal compact and the rule of law.
5. **Subsequent Obstruction & Current Deadlock:**
   - The Punjab government subsequently enacted another legislation (*Punjab Land Transfer of Property/Denotification Act*) denotifying the land acquired for the canal, returning land to original owners, and returning cheques to Haryana.
   - The Supreme Court directed Punjab to maintain *status quo* and construct the canal.
   - *Federal Lesson:* Both in the North (Punjab in SYL) and in the South (Karnataka in Cauvery), **states have openly defied Supreme Court directives**, pointing to a profound crisis of compliance in Indian federalism.

---

### 2. Examples Where the Tribunal System Worked: The Role of the Union

There are rare instances where water disputes were successfully resolved because of a **positive, proactive role played by the Union Government**:

1. **Narmada Water Dispute (Narmada Water Disputes Tribunal):**
   - Solved successfully because the riparian states (Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, and Rajasthan) **first reached an agreed political solution/consensus**, and the tribunal was subsequently utilized to formalize and legally operationalize the agreement.
2. **Krishna Water Dispute:**
   - The Krishna Tribunal had to be constituted twice (KWDT-I under Justice Bachawat, KWDT-II under Justice Brijesh Kumar).
   - While the first award was largely accepted, new and complex challenges emerged following the **bifurcation of Andhra Pradesh in 2014**, creating dispute dynamics among four riparian states: **Maharashtra (MH), Karnataka (KR), Andhra Pradesh (AP), and Telangana**.

Even during peacetime and normal circumstances (without an Emergency), the Union Parliament can legislate on entries in the State List under four specific provisions:

1. **Article 249 (Rajya Sabha Resolution in National Interest):**
   - If Rajya Sabha passes a resolution supported by not less than **$2/3^{\text{rd}}$ of members present and voting** declaring that it is necessary in the national interest for Parliament to make laws on a State subject.
   - *Safeguard:* Law remains valid for a maximum of 1 year at a time.
   - *State Concern:* Rajya Sabha is **hardly a true federal chamber** in real political practice:
     - Seats are distributed based on population, not equal state representation (unlike the US Senate).
     - The 2003 amendment to the Representation of the People Act removed the domicile requirement for Rajya Sabha members.
     - Party whips dominate Rajya Sabha voting rather than state interests.
2. **Article 252 (Legislation by Consent of Two or More States):**
   - If the legislatures of two or more states pass resolutions requesting Parliament to enact a law on a State List subject.
   - *The Danger of "Permanent Transfer":*
     - The transfer of legislative power w.r.t. these consenting states is of a **permanent nature**.
     - Once Parliament enacts such a law, **the State Legislatures lose the power to amend or repeal it**. Any amendment or repeal can be done **only by the Union Parliament**.
     - If an incoming state government of a different political party wishes to pursue a different socio-economic policy on that subject, it cannot do so!
3. **Article 253 (Legislation to Give Effect to International Agreements):**
   - Parliament has the power to make any law for the whole or any part of India for implementing any international treaty, agreement, or convention.
   - The ordinary distribution of powers between Centre and States **does not apply** (non-obstante clause).
   - *State Concern:*
     - Historically, international treaties were restricted to strategic, foreign policy, and defense issues.
     - Today, international treaties and multilateral regimes (e.g., WTO, TRIPS, climate change, biodiversity treaties) encompass **core state subjects** like agriculture, water, health, and land.
     - *Friction:* The Union Government enters into international commitments **without prior consultation with the State Governments**, yet requires states to implement the domestic costs.
4. **Article 200 & Article 201 (Reservation of State Bills for Presidential Consideration):**
   - The Governor has the discretionary power to reserve state legislative bills for the consideration of the President.
   - *Grounds of Misuse:*
     - Frequently exercised to **sabotage the legislative agenda of opposition-ruled states** in the political interest of the ruling party at the Centre.
     - Arbitrary exercise of power: Similar bills enacted in a Centre-ruled state are approved, while in an opposition-ruled state they are reserved or vetoed.
     - **No Time Limit:** There is no constitutional deadline within which the Governor or the President must give or withhold assent (effectively an absolute **pocket veto**).

### 3. Problems with the Union's Power to Issue Directions

- **Articles 256 & 257:**
  - The Union has executive power to issue binding directions to State governments regarding:
    - Compliance with Parliamentary laws (Art 256).
    - Ensuring the executive power of the State does not impede or prejudice Union executive power (Art 257).
    - Construction and maintenance of means of communication of national or military importance, and protection of railways.
- **The Coercive Sanction under Article 365:**
  - The Union does not just issue directions; it possesses the constitutional power to **punish non-compliant states**.
  - Under **Article 365**, where any State has failed to comply with, or give effect to, any directions given by the Union, it shall be lawful for the President to hold that a situation has arisen in which the government of the State **cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of this Constitution** (triggering Article 356).
  - *Consequence:* Failure to follow directions can lead to the **dismissal of the elected State government and dissolution of the State Legislative Assembly**.
  - *Critique:* These provisions were intended as emergency safeguards for extreme situations; they must be operated strictly in accordance with the **spirit of federalism and parliamentary democracy**.

### 4. Controversy Related to Article 356 (President's Rule)

#### What is Article 356?
- Proclamation of **President's Rule** in case of failure of constitutional machinery in a State.

#### Nature and Origin of Article 356:
1. **Unique and Aberrant Feature:** It is a unique, extraordinary feature of the Indian federation with no parallel in classic democratic federations like the USA or Australia.
2. **Colonial Legacy:** It is a direct colonial descendant of **Section 93 of the Government of India Act, 1935**, which empowered British Governors to assume absolute control of provinces.

#### Position in the Constituent Assembly:
- Many members (e.g., H.N. Kunzru, Kamath, Naziruddin Ahmad) strongly objected to the inclusion of Article 356, warning it would be abused to destroy provincial autonomy.
- **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar’s Famous Defense:**
  - Dr. Ambedkar pacified the assembly by assuring members that Article 356 was introduced purely as an extreme **safeguard and precaution**.
  - He famously declared that he hoped and expected such articles would **remain a "dead letter"** and would only be invoked as a weapon of last resort.

#### Actual Constitutional Practice:
- Far from remaining a dead letter, in less than 70 years of the existence of the Republic, Article 356 has been invoked and abused **more than 100 times**!

### 1. Historical Manners of Abuse of Article 356

- Article 356 has repeatedly been exercised in an **arbitrary, partisan manner** in the narrow political interest of the ruling party at the Centre:
  - **The 1977 Precedent:** When the Janata Party came to power at the Centre, it dissolved the legislative assemblies of **9 Congress-ruled states** in a single sweep, on the spurious political ground that the Congress had lost its popular mandate in the Lok Sabha elections.
  - **The 1980 Retaliation:** When Mrs. Indira Gandhi returned to power in 1980, she repeated the exact same unconstitutional act, dismissing the assemblies of **9 non-Congress ruled states** on identical political grounds.

---

### 2. Grave Consequences of the Misuse of Article 356

- Misuse of Article 356 has **undermined the federal spirit** of the Constitution and eroded democratic legitimacy.
- Crucially, it has **threatened national security and national integration**:
  - The arbitrary dismissal of elected state governments and the suppression of legitimate regional political expression was a **primary driver for the growth of alienation, violent militancy, and insurgency in sensitive border states like Jammu & Kashmir and Punjab**.

---

### 3. What is the Current Status of Article 356?

- In recent decades, there has been a **marked reduction in the misuse of Article 356** due to three balancing factors:
  1. **Judicial Activism:** Stringent review established by the higher judiciary.
  2. **Presidential Activism:** Presidents asserting their independent constitutional conscience (e.g., President K.R. Narayanan returning cabinet recommendations to impose President’s Rule in Uttar Pradesh in 1997 and Bihar in 1998).
  3. **Era of Coalition Politics:** Multi-party federal coalitions at the Centre made arbitrary dismissals politically costly.
- *However,* misuse has not been entirely eliminated, as seen in the controversial and unconstitutional invocations in **Arunachal Pradesh (2016)** and **Uttarakhand (2016)**.

---

### 4. Position of the Supreme Court: *S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)*

In the historic 9-judge bench decision in ***S.R. Bommai (1994)***, the Supreme Court declared:
> **Federalism is an integral component of the Basic Structure of the Indian Constitution, and not just a matter of administrative convenience.**

#### Stringent Conditions Laid Down for the Exercise of Power under Article 356:
1. **Scope of Judicial Review under Article 74(2):**
   - While the Court is barred by Article 74(2) from inquiring into *what advice* was tendered by ministers to the President, the Court **can require the Union to produce the material evidence** on the basis of which the President formed the satisfaction.
2. **Standard of Material Evidence ("Speaking Document"):**
   - The proclamation cannot be based on mere subjective whim or confidential political reports.
   - The material evidence must be a **"speaking document"** (clear, objective, and verifiable facts) that would compel any rational, reasonable person to conclude that the government of the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution.
3. **Prohibition of Immediate Assembly Dissolution (Suspended Animation):**
   - The State Legislative Assembly **cannot be dissolved immediately** upon the issuance of the Presidential proclamation.
   - The assembly must merely be kept in **suspended animation** until the proclamation is formally approved by **both Houses of Parliament** within two months (Article 356(3)).

### 2. Constitutional Position of the Governor

- **Head of the State Executive:**
  - Under **Article 153 and Article 154**, the Governor is the constitutional head of the State Executive, parallel to the President as the head of the Union Executive (Article 52/53).
  - Just as in the Union, where all executive actions are formally taken in the name of the President (Article 77), in the State **all executive actions are taken in the name of the Governor (Article 166)**.

### 3. What is the Need for the Governor?

1. **Parliamentary Form of Government at the State Level:**
   - India adopted the Westminster parliamentary system at both Union and State levels.
   - The parliamentary form of government inherently requires **two heads**:
     1. **Titular / Nominal Head:** President at the Centre; Governor in the State.
     2. **Real Executive Head:** Prime Minister at the Centre; Chief Minister in the State.
2. **Peculiar Dual Situation in India:**
   - India is not only a parliamentary democracy; India is also a **federation**.
   - *Which type of federation?* **Cooperative Federalism**.
   - *What is the uniqueness of Indian federalism?* **Interlocking federalism**, where the jurisdictions of the Union and the States are deeply intertwined rather than strictly separated.
