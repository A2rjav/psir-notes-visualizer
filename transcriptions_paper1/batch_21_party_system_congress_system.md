### PART A: Concluding Dimensions of Ethnicity & Conflict Management (Pages 145–146)

#### 1. Why Indian Diversity Does Not Lead to Balkanisation
*   **The Overlapping / Cross-Cutting Cleavages Thesis (James Manor):**  
    Unlike societies characterized by **cumulative / reinforcing cleavages** (such as Northern Ireland or pre-breakup Yugoslavia, where religion, language, alphabet, and historical grievances all fall along identical demographic fault lines), India's cleavages are **cross-cutting**. A citizen's linguistic identity (e.g., Tamil, Bengali) cuts across religious identity; religious identity cuts across caste lines; caste cuts across regional affiliations.
*   **Circumstantial Activation of Ethnicity:**  
    As Shubhra Ranjan notes, *"Indians choose ethnicity according to the circumstances."* Because an individual possesses multiple contextual identities, no single identity hardens into an permanent, all-encompassing political boundary, preventing everlasting fault lines.
*   **S.D. Muni's Mosaic Model:**  
    S.D. Muni describes India as a *"complex and colourful mosaic."* Indians do not possess a unidimensional, singular identity; rather, Indian citizenship is structurally characterized by **multilayered, nested identities** (Local $\rightarrow$ Caste/Sub-caste $\rightarrow$ Linguistic/Regional $\rightarrow$ Pan-Indian National).

#### 2. Theoretical Explanations for Ethnic Politics in India
The handwritten lecture synthesizes five major scholarly interpretations of ethnic mobilization:
1.  **Modernisation Backlash (Rajni Kothari):**
    *   Ethnic assertion is not a primitive remnant of an archaic past; rather, it is a **direct reactive consequence of excessive modernisation and the homogenising, centralising drive of the modern state**.
    *   The modern nation-state attempts to impose universalist, uniform, bureaucratic norms, which communities experience as cultural flattening and political alienation. Ethnic resurgence is thus a defensive counter-mobilisation against the state's cultural hegemony.
2.  **Political Construction & Globalisation (Neera Chandhoke):**
    *   In *Beyond Secularism*, Neera Chandhoke argues that **ethnic identities are not primordial givens**; they are historically contingent constructs produced by the specific nature of post-colonial political competition.
    *   In the contemporary era, **Globalisation** has accelerated this phenomenon: economic dislocation, spatial inequality, and cultural insecurity lead disempowered groups to seek solace and bargaining power in ethnic enclaves.
3.  **Patronage Democracy & Ethnic Headcounts (Kanchan Chandra):**
    *   In her seminal work *Why Ethnic Parties Succeed: Patronage and Ethnic Head Counts in India* (2004), Kanchan Chandra offers a rational-choice institutionalist critique:
    *   In a developing **"patronage democracy"** where the state monopolizes access to jobs, licenses, welfare transfers, and physical protection, and where institutional information is opaque, voters use ethnicity as a **low-cost informational shortcut (heuristic)** to predict which politician will deliver targeted material benefits to their group.
    *   Parties succeed when they conduct an **ethnic headcount**, demonstrating that their demographic coalition has the numerical leverage to capture power and dispense patronage.
4.  **Politics of Recognition, Dignity & Justice (Charles Taylor):**
    *   In *Multiculturalism and "The Politics of Recognition"* (1992), Charles Taylor demonstrates that human identity is dialogically created. Withholding recognition or projecting a demeaning, misrecognized image onto a group constitutes a severe form of oppression.
    *   Ethnic movements in India (such as Dalit assertion, tribal autonomy in Jharkhand/Bodoland, or Dravidian linguistic self-respect) are driven fundamentally by an existential quest for **dignity, moral equality, and social justice**, rather than mere economic appetites.
5.  **Composite Deprivation (Sarkaria Commission Report, 1988):**
    *   The Justice R.S. Sarkaria Commission noted that sub-national movements in India are rarely motivated by pure ethnic chauvinism alone. Instead, they represent a volatile blend of **economic underdevelopment, regional disparity, and political disenfranchisement** (e.g., neglect of infrastructure, resource extraction without local benefit, and unrepresentative political structures).

#### 3. Paul Brass: The Three Levels of Ethnic Conflict
In *Ethnic Groups and the State* (1985) and *Language, Religion and Politics in North India* (1974), Paul Brass formulates an elite-competition model. Ethnic identity is forged through conflict operating simultaneously across three concentric rings:
1.  **Struggle within the Ethnic Group (Intra-Group Hegemony):** Elites within a community compete to define the group's "authentic" boundaries, orthodox values, and legitimate leadership (e.g., moderate Akalis vs. militant factions in Punjab; competing factions within the Naga movement).
2.  **Competition between Ethnic Groups (Inter-Group Rivalry):** Rival communities compete for scarce economic opportunities, civil service quotas, university admissions, and localized political dominance (e.g., Bodos vs. Bengali Muslims in Assam; Meiteis vs. Kukis in Manipur; Assamese vs. Bengalis).
3.  **Competition between the Ethnic Group and the State (Sub-Nationalism vs. Sovereign Centre):** The organized ethnic community confronts the central government, demanding greater regional autonomy, constitutional recognition, asymmetrical privileges, or separate statehood.

#### 4. Institutional & Democratic Solutions to Ethnicity
*   **Democratic Decentralisation (Sarkaria & Punchhi Commissions):**  
    Both the Sarkaria Commission (1988) and the Justice Madan Mohan Punchhi Commission (2010) concluded that excessive centralisation fuels secessionism. The constitutional antidote is robust **functional, financial, and administrative decentralisation** (empowering local Panchayati Raj institutions, Autonomous District Councils under the Sixth Schedule, and state governments) to bring governance closer to cultural peripheries.
*   **Atul Kohli's Paradox: Democracy as Cause and Solution:**  
    *   In *"Can Democracies Accommodate Ethnic Movements?"* (1997), Atul Kohli identifies the foundational paradox of democratization in developing countries:
    *   *Why Democracy Causes the Problem:* The sudden introduction of competitive electoral democracy into traditional, transitional ("prismatic", following Fred Riggs) societies destabilizes ancient patron-client hierarchies. Threatened traditional elites, anxious about losing their hereditary status, mobilize their communities along primordial ethnic, linguistic, and caste lines to guarantee captive voting blocs.
    *   *Why Democracy is the Only Solution:* Coercive military suppression merely drives ethnic grievances underground, producing intractable insurgencies. Only open, responsive democratic negotiation can co-opt rebellious elites into the constitutional fold.
    *   *Nehru's Linguistic Accommodation:* Kohli celebrates Jawaharlal Nehru's accommodation of the States Reorganisation Act (1956) based on language. By accommodating Tamil and Telugu linguistic pride within a flexible federal union, Nehru defused potential secessionism.
    *   *Kohli's Prescriptive Maxim:* *"India has a lot of democracy, but not the right type of democracy. We need not less democracy, but more democracy."*
*   **Ashutosh Varshney: Inter-Communal Civic Networks:**  
    *   In *Ethnic Conflict and Civic Life: Hindus and Muslims in India* (2002), Ashutosh Varshney investigates why communal violence recurs in certain Indian cities (e.g., Ahmedabad, Aligarh) while other cities with identical demographic ratios remain peaceful (e.g., Surat prior to 1992, Calicut, Lucknow).
    *   *The Core Variable:* The presence of **inter-ethnic / inter-communal civic networks**. Where civic associations are integrated across communal lines—spanning **associational networks** (integrated trade unions, business associations, professional clubs, sports teams) and **quotidian engagement** (families eating together, children playing together, celebrating festivals jointly)—rumors are swiftly debunked, peace committees function, and politicians cannot engineer polarizing ethnic riots for electoral gain. Where civil society is segregated along ethnic lines, communal friction escalates uncontrollably.

#### 1. Stasiology: Definition & The Normative Debate
*   **Stasiology:** Defined as the scientific, comparative, and sociological study of political parties, party systems, and electoral dynamics (coined by French political scientist **Maurice Duverger** in his 1951 foundational work *Les Partis Politiques*).
*   **The Liberal-Democratic Defense (Harold Laski):**  
    In *Parliamentary Government in England* (1938) and *A Grammar of Politics* (1925), Harold Laski argued that **representative democracy is inconceivable without political parties**. Parties perform indispensable systemic functions:
    1.  Aggregating disparate social interests into coherent national programs.
    2.  Forming the government and maintaining stable legislative majorities.
    3.  Providing a constitutionally institutionalized, loyal opposition to hold executive power accountable.
    4.  Crystallizing and educating public opinion.
    5.  Acting as vehicles for political recruitment, modern education, and civic socialisation.
*   **The Anti-Party Tradition:**
    *   *George Washington:* In his famous 1796 *Farewell Address*, Washington issued a grave warning against the *"baneful effects of the spirit of party,"* cautioning that political parties serve to organize factions, stimulate vindictive animosities, and open the door to foreign influence and domestic despotism.
    *   *Mahatma Gandhi:* Believed conventional party competition fosters factionalism, divisiveness, and corruption in village life. In his final document (the draft constitution for the Congress, often called his *Last Will and Testament* of 30 January 1948), Gandhi advocated disbanding the Indian National Congress as an electoral vehicle and transforming it into a **Lok Sevak Sangh** dedicated to non-political constructive social work.
    *   *M.N. Roy's Radical Humanism:* Denounced party politics as an immoral scramble for power that treats individual citizens as passive voting cattle. Roy advocated **"Partyless Democracy"**, structured upon autonomous, decentralized **People's Committees** exercising direct sovereign control without parliamentary parties.
    *   *Jayaprakash Narayan (JP) & Sarvodaya:* Re-articulated the vision of a **Partyless Democracy** based on consensual, participatory village self-governance (*Gram Swaraj*), arguing that party machines divide communities along artificial party lines, paralyzing genuine social solidarity.

#### 2. Concept of "Party System" vs. Number of Parties
*   **Systemic Relevance (Giovanni Sartori & Maurice Duverger):**  
    A party system is **not defined merely by counting the sheer number of registered political parties**. Rather, following Giovanni Sartori's classic formulation in *Parties and Party Systems: A Framework for Analysis* (1976), a party is systemically relevant only if it possesses:
    1.  **Coalition Potential:** Its parliamentary support is essential to form or sustain a governing coalition.
    2.  **Blackmail Potential (Intimidation / Veto Power):** Its capacity to alter the direction of political competition and force mainstream parties to adjust their ideological positions.
*   **Application to India:**  
    Although hundreds of political parties existed in India at independence (Socialist Party, Kisan Mazdoor Praja Party, Bharatiya Jana Sangh, CPI, Hindu Mahasabha), the system operated until 1967 as a **One-Party Dominant System** (or "Congress System"), because only the Indian National Congress possessed systemic governing relevance across both the Union and the States.

1.  **Rajni Kothari's Bifurcated Character:**  
    Indian parties combine formal **Western structural characteristics** (constitutions, central committees, election manifestos, parliamentary whips) with entrenched **non-Western behavioral practices** (absence of internal elections, high-command culture, personalized leadership cults, and complete conflation between party organizational bosses and state governmental authority).
2.  **Zoya Hasan's Pathological Features of Indian Parties:**  
    In *Parties and Party Politics in India* and *Quest for Power*, Zoya Hasan identifies four defining pathologies:
    *   *Absence of Ideology ('Catch-All' Nature):* Parties have ceased to be ideological vehicles representing distinct class interests. They operate as opportunistic "catch-all" electoral cartels (borrowing Otto Kirchheimer's concept), pitching vague populist slogans to all social strata simultaneously.
    *   *Popular Disconnect & Corporate Nexus:* Contemporary parties are increasingly disconnected from their organic popular bases, relying instead on substantial corporate funding. *The fall of the Left Front in West Bengal (2011)* after 34 years of rule is a classic case study: the CPI(M)-led government alienated its historic rural peasant base in Singur and Nandigram by deploying state force to acquire agricultural land on behalf of corporate conglomerates (e.g., Tata Motors).
    *   *Idiosyncratic Nomenclature:* Indian parties often bear unusual names that reflect historical personalism or linguistic regionalism rather than programmatic philosophies (e.g., *AIADMK*—All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, named after C.N. Annadurai; *Jammu & Kashmir National Conference*; *Biju Janata Dal*, named after Biju Patnaik).
    *   *Ideological Convergence & Absence of Grand Political Design:* Beneath rhetorical posturing, virtually all mainstream Indian parties execute an identical developmental playbook: neoliberal economic incentives combined with targeted, non-universal populist patronage (*Revadi* culture). None articulates an alternative philosophical blueprint for the socioeconomic restructuring of society.
3.  **Paul Brass's Three Core Paradoxes:**  
    In *The Politics of India Since Independence* (1990), Paul Brass highlights three contradictory realities:
    *   *Paradox I: Modern Bureaucracy vs. Patrimonial Dynasties:* Structurally, parties are registered as modern rational-legal organizations with elaborate formal hierarchies. In practice, power is monopolized by dynastic families treating parties as private familial trusts (e.g., Congress, SP, RJD, DMK, TDP, Shiv Sena).
    *   *Paradox II: Products of Modernisation Deploying Primordial Identity:* Parties are themselves historical creations of modern representative institutions, yet their primary electoral mobilization strategies rely on pre-modern, traditional identities—caste arithmetic, sub-caste polarization, religious consolidation, and linguistic sub-nationalism.
    *   *Paradox III: Indispensable Pillars of Democracy Bereft of Internal Democracy:* Political parties are recognized as the absolute foundation of competitive parliamentary democracy in India, yet internally they operate as centralized autocracies where party presidents nominate executive bodies and internal dissent is stamped out as anti-party activity.
4.  **Prof. Yogendra Yadav's Twin Diagnoses:**
    *   *Institutionalisation vs. De-institutionalisation:* Indian parties are undergoing a contradictory dual trajectory. On the one hand, their geographical footprint, financial apparatus, digital reach, and electoral machinery are unprecedentedly expansive (**institutionalisation**). On the other hand, the emotional loyalty, ideological commitment, and organizational depth of their cadres and followers are rapidly hollowing out (**de-institutionalisation**).
    *   *Reduction to Instrumental Vote-Harvesting Machines:* Parties have abandoned the historic mission of **Nation-Building** (social reform, mass education, inter-communal fraternity, moral leadership) that defined the pre-independence freedom movement. Instead, their functional purpose has been degraded to the purely instrumental mechanic of managing election logistics, engineering polling coalitions, and capturing executive office.

#### 1. Concept of "One-Party Dominance" (W.H. Morris-Jones)
In *The Government and Politics of India* (1964), **W.H. Morris-Jones** coined the term **"One-Party Dominant System"** to categorize India's unique post-independence political architecture.
*   **Crucial Distinction from Authoritarian One-Party States:**
    *   *One-Party Socialist/Authoritarian State (e.g., USSR, China):* The state constitutionally establishes a legal monopoly for a single official vanguard party. Opposition parties are legally banned, alternative ideologies are suppressed as treason, and political competition is outlawed.
    *   *One-Party Dominant System (India):* Operates within a fully democratic, competitive constitutional framework. Universal adult suffrage, freedom of the press, fundamental rights, an independent judiciary, and a non-partisan Election Commission exist. Multiple opposition parties freely contest elections across all ideological spectrums (Far-Left Communists, Centrist Socialists, Free-Market Swatantra, Right-Wing Bharatiya Jana Sangh). Yet, one preeminent party repeatedly sweeps elections and retains hegemonic control over state apparatus **through democratic consent and open electoral victory**.

#### 2. Rajni Kothari's Seminal Model: "The Congress System"
In his path-breaking essay *"The Congress 'System' in India"* (Asian Survey, December 1964) and subsequent book *Politics in India* (1970), **Rajni Kothari** provided the definitive theoretical conceptualization of this era:
1.  **The Hegemonic Party as a "Grand Coalition" / "Rainbow Coalition":**  
    The Congress was not a conventional, ideologically dogmatic political party. It was a massive, open-ended **umbrella movement** that had evolved during the anti-colonial freedom struggle. It functioned as a mini-parliament, accommodating landlords and landless peasants, industrialists and trade unionists, traditional conservatives and secular modernists, upper castes and subalterns into a grand strategic equilibrium.
2.  **Party of Consensus vs. Parties of Pressure:**
    *   *The Party of Consensus:* The Congress occupied the expansive, legitimate center of the political arena. It synthesized differing societal demands, internalizing conflicts and arriving at consensual programmatic solutions.
    *   *Parties of Pressure:* Opposition parties (with the brief exception of the 1957 E.M.S. Namboodiripad-led Communist ministry in Kerala) stood no realistic prospect of dislodging Congress from government, either at the national center or across the states. Instead, they functioned on the **"margins" / periphery** as **pressure groups**. They mobilized public agitation around specific issues (e.g., language reorganization, cow protection, socialist land ceilings), exerting latent pressure on the Congress core.
3.  **Intra-Party Factions as Built-in Correctives & Thermostats:**
    *   Because the external opposition was weak, **the primary opposition to government policies operated from inside the Congress itself**.
    *   Congress contained distinct internal ideological factions (e.g., Nehruvian Democratic Socialists like K.D. Malaviya and Krishna Menon vs. Conservative Right-Wingers like Morarji Desai, Sardar Patel, and S.K. Patil).
    *   These factions engaged in continuous bargaining, factional rivalry, and internal elections. Whenever a government policy tilted too far in one direction, opposing factions within the party mobilized, forcing the leadership to self-correct. Thus, **intra-party factionalism served as an internal democratic equilibrator**.
4.  **Democratic Safeguards within Dominance:**  
    Despite enjoying absolute legislative hegemony, the Congress System avoided authoritarianism because:
    *   External opposition parties, though numerically small, retained high moral and intellectual stature in Parliament (e.g., Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Ram Manohar Lohia, J.B. Kripalani, Shyama Prasad Mukherjee, A.K. Gopalan, Atal Bihari Vajpayee).
    *   The Congress leadership showed scrupulous respect for parliamentary debate, minority rights, and institutional propriety.

#### 3. Four Foundational Pillars of Congress Hegemony
Shubhra Ranjan's lecture delineates four structural factors that sustained the Congress System:
1.  **Moral Capital & Legacy of the National Movement:**  
    Congress was universally recognized by the Indian electorate as the legitimate vehicle that defeated the British Raj and won national independence. Voting for Congress was perceived by millions as an act of patriotic duty.
2.  **Mahatma Gandhi's Grassroots Organizational Architecture:**  
    Under Gandhi's leadership following the 1920 Nagpur session, Congress had transformed from an elite debating society into a mass party with deep organizational roots down to the village level (**PCCs**—Pradesh Congress Committees; **DCCs**—District Congress Committees; and **Mandal Committees**). This provided an unmatched electoral transmission belt.
3.  **Centrist Ideology & Rejection of Extremes:**  
    Congress positioned itself squarely in the ideological center. By embracing a **"Socialistic Pattern of Society"** (1955 Avadi Resolution) while protecting private property and democratic freedoms, it effectively neutralized both revolutionary communism on the left and sectarian communalism on the right.
4.  **Pt. Jawaharlal Nehru's Democratic Consensual Leadership:**  
    Nehru's leadership provided the moral and intellectual mortar holding the diverse system together:
    *   *(a) Democratic Intellectual Climate:* Encouraged vigorous, open ideological debate within the Union Cabinet and Congress Working Committee (CWC).
    *   *(b) Culture of Toleration & Free Speech:* Embraced constructive dissent, writing regular personal letters to Chief Ministers to build consensus rather than issuing authoritarian diktats.
    *   *(c) Accommodative Statecraft:* Consistently accommodated regional, linguistic, and agrarian interests (e.g., linguistic reorganization of states).
    *   *(d) Neutralisation of Social Cleavages:* Balanced upper-caste administrative elites with emerging agrarian intermediate castes through patronage and land reforms (abolition of Zamindari).
    *   *(e) Sensitivity Towards Minorities:* Passionately defended secularism, ensuring Muslims and other minorities felt constitutionally protected in post-partition India.
    *   *The Indispensable Nation-Builder:* Nehru successfully convinced the mass public that the institutional integrity of the Congress party was indispensable for the survival of India as a sovereign, united nation.

#### 4. Institutional Anchors of Democratic Integrity
The lecture notes emphasize two structural guarantees that prevented the Congress System from degenerating into an autocracy:
1.  **Nehru's Deep Personal Commitment to Constitutional Democracy:**  
    Nehru treated Parliament with reverence, attended debates punctually, accepted judicial independence even when courts struck down early welfare legislation, and refused to use his immense personal popularity to undermine democratic norms (exemplified by his famous 1937 pseudonymous essay *Rashtrapati* in *The Modern Review*, where writing under the pen-name 'Chanakya', he warned the public against giving Jawaharlal Nehru dictatorial power).
2.  **The Independent Election Commission of India (ECI):**  
    Under India's first Chief Election Commissioner, **Sukumar Sen**, the Election Commission established an unimpeachable precedent of impartiality, logistics excellence, and structural autonomy during the 1951–52 First General Elections. By administering universal adult franchise across a predominantly illiterate electorate with flawless transparency, the ECI institutionalized competitive democratic legitimacy.

#### 1. The 1967 Fourth General Elections: The Great Watershed
*   The breakdown of the Congress System erupted during the **Fourth General Elections of 1967**, often described as India's first great electoral earthquake.
*   *The Electoral Collapse:* While Congress retained a razor-thin majority at the Centre (283 out of 520 seats, a sharp drop from 361 in 1962), it **lost power across eight major Indian states** (Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, West Bengal, Orissa, Madras/Tamil Nadu, and Kerala). In a ninth state (Rajasthan), it was temporarily unable to form a government without engineering defections.
*   *Rise of Non-Congress Coalitions (Samyukta Vidhayak Dal - SVD):* Heterogeneous, anti-Congress opposition coalitions (comprising Socialists, Jana Sangh, Swatantra, and agrarian peasant parties like Charan Singh's Bharatiya Kranti Dal) formed state governments under the banner of **SVD**. In Tamil Nadu, the DMK won an outright majority, inaugurating the permanent regionalization of Dravidian politics.
*   *Structural Causes of the 1967 Shock:*
    1.  Passing of towering consensus leaders: Death of Jawaharlal Nehru (1964) and Lal Bahadur Shastri (1966).
    2.  Severe economic distress: Consecutive monsoon failures, catastrophic food shortages, devaluation of the Rupee (1966), and rising inflation.
    3.  Agrarian assertion: The newly enriched intermediate peasant castes (OBCs/kulaks born out of early Zamindari abolition) demanded political power commensurate with their economic muscle, breaking free from the upper-caste-dominated Congress machine.
    4.  Ram Manohar Lohia's strategy of **Non-Congressism (*Gair-Congressvad*)**: Uniting ideologically disparate opposition parties on a single platform to prevent the division of anti-Congress votes under the First-Past-The-Post system.

#### 2. Suhas Palshikar's Three-Stage Analytical Life-Cycle of Congress
Eminent political scientist **Prof. Suhas Palshikar** provides a comprehensive chronological and conceptual periodization of the Indian National Congress:
1.  **Phase I: The Phase of Hegemony (1947 to 1967):**  
    Identical to Rajni Kothari's Congress System. An era of consensual governance, overarching umbrella legitimacy, and internal factional accommodation.
2.  **Phase II: The Phase of Confrontation (1967 to 1989):**  
    Marked by bitter institutional confrontations between the ruling party and the judiciary, federal states, civil society, and opposition alliances. The party dismantled its consensual machinery, leading to the 1969 party split, the populist *Garibi Hatao* rhetoric of 1971, the authoritarian imposition of the Internal Emergency (1975–1977), the Janata Party interregnum, and the violent centralisation of the 1980s (Punjab, Kashmir, Assam crises).
3.  **Phase III: The Phase of Survival (1989 to Present):**  
    Inaugurated by the 1989 general elections and the rise of the **"Third Electoral System"** (Yogendra Yadav). Congress lost its pan-Indian dominance, forced to survive as one of several competing poles in an era of coalition federalism.
    *   *The 2014 Verdict as "Demise":* According to Palshikar, the **2014 general election results did not signify a routine electoral decline, but the historic "demise" of the Congress party**. Congress was reduced to an unprecedented low of **44 seats**—drastically lower even than its 1977 post-Emergency tally (154 seats). The structural hegemony of the Congress was definitively dismantled, paving the way for a **Second Dominant Party System** centered around the Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP).

#### 3. Internal Pathologies & De-Institutionalisation under Indira Gandhi
The transition from Phase I to Phase II was catalyzed by the systemic **de-institutionalisation** of the Congress party under Mrs. Indira Gandhi:
1.  **Extreme Personalisation & Centralisation of Power:**  
    Following the 1969 split (Congress (R) vs. Congress (O) / Syndicate), Indira Gandhi bypassed the state party barons and concentrated absolute power in the Prime Minister's Secretariat (PMO) and her personal family circle. The collective party decision-making bodies (CWC, Central Parliamentary Board) became toothless rubber stamps.
2.  **Erosion of Intra-Party Democracy:**  
    The consensual, federal bargaining culture of the Nehru era was systematically extinguished. Chief Ministers and Pradesh Congress Committee (PCC) presidents were no longer elected by state legislators; they were arbitrary **high-command appointees** dispatched from New Delhi, creating a culture of chronic vulnerability and sycophancy (*"Indira is India, India is Indira"*).
3.  **Suspension of Organizational Elections Since 1972:**  
    As explicitly documented in the handwritten notes, **internal organizational elections were completely abandoned in 1972**. The organic link between party cadres and grassroots voters was severed. Instead of an institutionalized cadre structure, the party came to rely exclusively on charismatic plebiscitary appeals by the supreme leader and mass populist rhetoric.
4.  **Decay of Dispute-Resolution Mechanisms:**  
    Because internal factions were no longer allowed to negotiate transparently through internal party forums, local leaders faced two stark choices: complete subservience to the high command or defection. This institutional rot spurred chronic floor-crossing (*Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram*), leading directly to the breakdown of constitutional stability in the states.

### Question 3 (UPSC CSE 2016, 15 Marks)
> *"Can democracies accommodate ethnic and subnational movements? Critically evaluate Atul Kohli's thesis in the light of the Indian experience."*

#### Comprehensive Model Answer Outline:
1.  **Introduction:**
    *   Contrast Western modernization theories (which predicted modernization would dissolve ethnic identities) with the post-colonial reality of rising ethnic assertions.
    *   Introduce **Atul Kohli's** seminal essay: *"Can Democracies Accommodate Ethnic Movements?"* (1997).
2.  **Kohli's Central Paradox: Democracy as Cause and Solution:**
    *   *The Cause:* In prismatic/transitional societies, competitive mass elections threaten traditional elites. Anxious about losing power, elites engineer identity-based mobilizations, stoking ethnic insecurities.
    *   *The Solution:* Coercive centralisation escalates violence; only responsive democratic negotiation can de-escalate confrontation along Kohli's **Inverted U-Curve**.
3.  **The Indian Empirical Record (Nehru vs. Indira Gandhi):**

    *   *Indira Gandhi's Centralisation:* Coercive meddling, dismissal of elected state governments, and political manipulation of religious cleavages in Punjab and Kashmir led to violent insurgencies.
4.  **Prescriptive Synthesis (Ashutosh Varshney & Punchhi Commission):**
    *   Accommodating ethnicity requires institutionalising **democratic decentralisation** (Sarkaria and Punchhi Commissions) and fostering **inter-communal civic networks** (Ashutosh Varshney) to prevent political manipulation.
5.  **Conclusion:**
    *   Conclude with Kohli's foundational maxim: *"India needs not less democracy, but more democracy"*—deepening substantive federal devolution and institutional accommodation is the ultimate safeguard of national unity.
