#### Comprehensive PSIR Scholarly Commentary on Page 168

1. **The Factual and Constitutional Milieu of *State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan* (1951 AIR 226):**
   - Srimathi Champakam Dorairajan, a Brahmin candidate, was denied admission to the Madras Medical College despite possessing superior marks, owing to the **Communal Government Order (G.O.) No. 1612**, issued by the Government of Madras in 1927 under the Justice Party administration. The Communal G.O. allocated seats across distinct communal quotas (Non-Brahmin Hindus, Brahmins, Backward Hindus, Muslims, Anglo-Indians/Indian Christians, and Depressed Classes).
   - Upon the commencement of the Constitution of India on 26 January 1950, Srimathi Dorairajan filed a writ petition under Article 32, pleading that the Communal G.O. violated her Fundamental Rights under **Article 14** (Equality before law), **Article 15(1)** (Prohibition of discrimination on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth), and especially **Article 29(2)** ("No citizen shall be denied admission into any educational institution maintained by the State or receiving aid out of State funds on grounds only of religion, race, caste, language or any of them").

2. **The Judicial Dilemma & The Clash of Constitutional Values:**
   - **The Defense of the State of Madras:** The Advocate-General of Madras argued that the Communal G.O. was enacted to fulfill the positive mandate of **Article 46** of Part IV (Directive Principles of State Policy), which commands: *"The State shall promote with special care the educational and economic interests of the weaker sections of the people, and, in particular, of the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes, and shall protect them from social injustice and all forms of exploitation."*
   - Furthermore, **Article 37** declares that Directive Principles are *"fundamental in the governance of the country and it shall be the duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws."*
   - **The Counter-Argument:** Conversely, **Article 13(2)** serves as the express constitutional sword of Judicial Review: *"The State shall not make any law which takes away or abridges the rights conferred by this Part and any law made in contravention of this clause shall, to the extent of the contravention, be void."* Coupled with **Article 32**, which Dr. B.R. Ambedkar termed the *"heart and soul of the Constitution"*, the Supreme Court was constitutionally mandated to act as the institutional sentinel on the *qui vive*.

3. **The Doctrinal Precedent: Subordination of Part IV to Part III:**
   - Justice Sudhi Ranjan Das, delivering the unanimous judgment of the 7-Judge Constitution Bench, established the initial doctrine of rigid hierarchical subordination:
     > *"The Directive Principles of State Policy, which by Article 37 are expressly made unenforceable by a Court, cannot override the provisions found in Part III which, notwithstanding other provisions, are expressly made enforceable by appropriate Writs, Orders or directions under Article 32. The Chapter of Fundamental Rights is sacrosanct and not liable to be abridged by any Legislative or Executive act or order, except to the extent provided in the appropriate Article in Part III. The Directive Principles of State Policy have to conform to and run as subsidiary to the Chapter of Fundamental Rights."*

4. **The "Creative Interpretation" and The Seed of Amending Power:**
   - The Court's verdict struck down the Communal G.O. as a violation of Article 29(2). However, recognizing that deep-seated social stratification in India made affirmative action an imperative of historical justice, the Court indicated that while an **ordinary legislative or executive order** could not bypass Part III, Parliament in its sovereign capacity could amend the Constitution itself.
   - This distinction between **ordinary law (*lex*)** and **constituent law (*jus*)** formed the cornerstone of early Indian constitutional adjudication.

#### Comprehensive PSIR Scholarly Commentary on Page 112

1. **The Executive & Legislative Retaliation: The First Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951:**
   - Shocked by the judicial invalidation of land reforms (e.g., *Kameshwar Singh v. State of Bihar*, where the Patna High Court struck down the Bihar Land Reforms Act, 1950) and educational affirmative action (*Champakam Dorairajan*), Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru's Provisional Parliament enacted the **1st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951** with breathtaking speed.
   - **Insertion of Article 15(4):** Overrode *Champakam Dorairajan* by providing: *"Nothing in this article or in clause (2) of Article 29 shall prevent the State from making any special provision for the advancement of any socially and educationally backward classes of citizens or for the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes."* This provided constitutional sanction for caste-based educational reservations.
   - **Insertion of Article 31A:** Saved laws providing for the acquisition of "estates" or modification of agrarian rights from challenges under Articles 14, 19, or 31.
   - **Insertion of Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule:** Created a constitutional vault or "black hole". Any Act placed within the Ninth Schedule was immunized retrospectively against judicial review on the ground of violating Part III.

2. **The First Battle over Constituent Power: *Shankari Prasad Singh Deo v. Union of India* (AIR 1951 SC 458):**
   - **The Petitioner's Contention:** Prominent zamindars (landowners) challenged the 1st Constitutional Amendment Act, arguing that Article 13(2) prohibits the State from making any "law" that abridges Fundamental Rights. Since a Constitutional Amendment Act is enacted by Parliament, it constitutes a "law" under Article 13(2) and is therefore void.
   - **The Unanimous 5-Judge Verdict (delivered by Chief Justice Harilal Kania, speaking through Justice Patanjali Sastri):**
     - The Supreme Court drew a sharp, classical distinction between **Ordinary Legislative Power** exercised by Parliament under Article 245/246 read with the legislative lists, and **Constituent Power** exercised under Article 368.
     - The word "law" in Article 13(2) must be read as referring to rules or regulations made in exercise of ordinary legislative power, not to constitutional amendments made in exercise of constituent power.
     - Hence, Parliament possessed the untrammeled power to amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights, under Article 368.

3. **Subsequent Challenges: The 4th Amendment and *Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan* (AIR 1965 SC 845):**
   - The **4th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1955** amended Article 31(2) to provide that the adequacy of compensation determined by law for acquisition of property could not be questioned in any court.
   - In *Sajjan Singh* (1965), the validity of the **17th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1964** (which added 44 state land reform acts to the Ninth Schedule and redefined "estate" to include ryotwari lands) was challenged before a 5-Judge Constitution Bench.
   - Chief Justice P.B. Gajendragadkar, speaking for the majority (3:2), reaffirmed *Shankari Prasad*, holding that the constituent power conferred by Article 368 is wide, plenary, and includes the power to take away or curtail Fundamental Rights.
   - **The Seeds of Dissent (The Intellectual Genesis of Basic Structure):**
     - **Justice Mohammad Hidayatullah:** Expressed grave misgivings about whether Fundamental Rights could be played with by a parliamentary majority: *"It is not easy to say that they are not fundamental... Our Constitution was not intended to be like a toy in the hands of the legislature."*
     - **Justice Janardan Raghunath Mudholkar:** Wondered whether the Indian Constitution possesses certain **"basic features"** that cannot be abrogated. He questioned whether Parliament could alter the republican character of the Constitution or replace the parliamentary system with a dictatorship. Justice Mudholkar explicitly credited the German jurist **Prof. Dieter Conrad** and Pakistan's Supreme Court jurisprudence for this line of inquiry.

#### Comprehensive PSIR Scholarly Commentary on Page 113

1. **The Political Earthquake of 1967: "The Downfall of the Congress System":**
   - The year 1967 represents a critical watershed in Indian politics, famously theorized by **Rajni Kothari** as the collapse / decline of the "One-Party Dominance System" or the **"Congress System"**.
   - In the 4th General Elections (February 1967), the Indian National Congress suffered massive electoral reverses: its Lok Sabha majority was reduced to a razor-thin 54% (283 out of 520 seats), and it lost power in 8 major Northern and Eastern states (the *Samyukta Vidhayak Dal* coalition governments across Punjab, Haryana, UP, Bihar, West Bengal, Orissa, MP, and Madras).
   - In this climate of political vulnerability, the institutional balance shifted dramatically. The Supreme Court asserted an unprecedented degree of constitutional supremacy over an executive that had lost its aura of electoral invincibility.

2. **The Landmark Verdict in *I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab* (AIR 1967 SC 1643):**
   - **The Case:** Henry and William Golaknath owned over 500 acres of farmland in Jalandhar, Punjab. Under the Punjab Security of Land Tenures Act, 1953, the state informed them that each brother could keep only 30 acres, with the surplus vested in the state. The Golaknaths petitioned the Supreme Court, challenging the 17th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1964, which placed the Punjab Act into the Ninth Schedule.
   - **The Bench:** An unprecedented 11-Judge Constitution Bench was constituted, presided over by Chief Justice **Koka Subba Rao**.
   - **The Majority Verdict (6:5):** By the narrowest of margins, Chief Justice Subba Rao, joined by Justices J.C. Shah, S.M. Sikri, J.M. Shelat, C.A. Vaidialingam, and Mohammad Hidayatullah (who authored a separate concurring opinion), overruled *Shankari Prasad* and *Sajjan Singh*.
   - **Key Doctrinal Findings:**
     1. **Article 368 Contains Only Procedure, Not Power:** The Court held that Article 368 merely outlined the *procedure* for amending the Constitution; the substantive *power* to amend was derived from the residuary legislative powers of Parliament under Article 248 read with Entry 97 of List I.
     2. **Constitutional Amendment is "Law" under Article 13(2):** An amendment to the Constitution is an exercise of legislative power. The term "law" in Article 13(2) includes both ordinary law and constitutional law. Therefore, if a constitutional amendment takes away or abridges any Fundamental Right in Part III, it is *ultra vires* and void.
     3. **Transcendental Position of Part III:** Fundamental Rights are given a "transcendental and primordial position" in the Indian constitutional architecture; they are natural rights preserved by the people against all state encroachment.

3. **The Doctrine of Prospective Overruling:**
   - Borrowing a concept from American jurisprudence (established by Justice Benjamin Cardozo in *Great Northern Railway v. Sunburst Oil & Refining Co.*), CJI Subba Rao introduced the **Doctrine of Prospective Overruling** to avoid constitutional chaos.
   - The Court declared that all previous amendments (1st, 4th, and 17th) remained valid for the past, but prospectively, from the date of the judgment (27 February 1967), Parliament possessed **zero power to abridge or take away any of the Fundamental Rights in Part III**.

4. **The Accusation of Judicial Activism and Imbalance:**
   - The *Golaknath* judgment was fiercely criticized by progressive scholars (such as Upendra Baxi, P.K. Tripathi, and Mohan Kumaramangalam) as an act of conservative, property-defending judicial overreach that locked the Constitution in a straightjacket, paralyzing agrarian land redistribution and social transformation.

5. **Parliament's Legislative Counter-Strike: The 24th Amendment Act, 1971:**
   - Following her landslide electoral triumph in the 1971 "Garibi Hatao" Lok Sabha elections, Prime Minister Indira Gandhi introduced a barrage of constitutional amendments to neutralize *Golaknath*.
   - **The 24th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971:**
     - Inserted **Article 13(4)**: *"Nothing in this article shall apply to any amendment of this Constitution made under Article 368."*
     - Inserted **Article 368(3)**: *"Nothing in Article 13 shall apply to any amendment made under this article."*
     - This explicit reciprocal cross-immunization sought to surgically decouple Article 13 from Article 368.

#### Comprehensive PSIR Scholarly Commentary on Page 114

1. **The Comprehensive Remodeling of Article 368 by the 24th Amendment:**
   - **Title Alteration:** The original marginal heading of Article 368 read: *"Procedure for amendment of the Constitution."* The 24th Amendment substituted it with: *"Power of Parliament to amend the Constitution and procedure therefor."*
   - **Explicit Grant of Constituent Power:** Added clause (1) to Article 368: *"Notwithstanding anything in this Constitution, Parliament may in exercise of its constituent power amend by way of addition, variation or repeal any provision of this Constitution in accordance with the procedure laid down in this article."*
   - **Mandatory Presidential Assent:** In clause (2), the phrase *"it shall be presented to the President who shall declare that he assents to the Bill or that he withholds assent therefrom"* was replaced by *"it shall be presented to the President who shall give his assent to the Bill"*. The President was stripped of all discretionary veto power regarding constitutional amendment bills.

2. **The 25th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971 & The Enactment of Article 31C:**
   - The 25th Amendment substituted the word "amount" for the word "compensation" in Article 31(2), completely neutralizing the Supreme Court's ruling in *R.C. Cooper v. Union of India* (Bank Nationalisation Case, 1970) which required market-value compensation.
   - **Insertion of Article 31C (The Epicenter of Constitutional Conflict):**
     - **First Part:** No law giving effect to the policy of the State towards securing the principles specified in clause (b) or clause (c) of **Article 39** shall be deemed to be void on the ground that it is inconsistent with, or takes away or abridges any of the rights conferred by **Article 14, Article 19, or Article 31**.
       - *Article 39(b):* Ownership and control of material resources of the community distributed to subserve the common good.
       - *Article 39(c):* Operation of the economic system does not result in the concentration of wealth and means of production to common detriment.

## Direct UPSC PSIR Exam Alignment & Model Answers

### Question 1 (UPSC PSIR 2019 / 2023 Archetype — 20 Marks / 250 Words)
**"The Basic Structure Doctrine has prevented India from sliding into authoritarianism, yet it remains vulnerable to the charge of judicial despotism and metaphysical overreach." Critically evaluate.**

#### Model Answer:

The Basic Structure Doctrine, formulated by a razor-thin 7:6 majority in *Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala* (1973), represents the supreme innovation of Indian constitutionalism. It resolved the existential dialectic between Westminster-style Parliamentary Sovereignty (A.V. Dicey) and American-style Constitutional Supremacy.

**1. The Salvation Thesis: Safeguard Against Authoritarianism**
As jurist Zia Mody observes in *10 Judgements That Changed India*, the doctrine proved to be a "blessing in disguise" in a practical political sense. 
- *Countering Executive Hegemony:* Enacted on the eve of the Internal Emergency (1975–77), the doctrine provided the constitutional ammunition in *Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain* (1975) to invalidate the draconian 39th Amendment (Article 329A(4)), which sought to place the Prime Minister's election beyond judicial scrutiny.
- *Protecting the Integrity of the Charter:* In *Minerva Mills* (1980), the Court checked the majoritarian excesses of the 42nd Amendment, invalidating clauses (4) and (5) of Article 368 that sought to confer unbridled, unlimited constituent power on Parliament and oust judicial review.
- *Preserving Post-Colonial Democracy:* Unlike neighboring post-colonial states in South Asia (Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka) which succumbed to constitutional breakdown and military despotism, India's Basic Structure doctrine anchored democratic continuity. As Mody profoundly notes: *"Uncertain democracy is preferable to certain tyranny."*

**2. The Critique: Judicial Despotism & Metaphysical Overreach**
Conversely, the doctrine faces rigorous theoretical critiques:
- *Counter-Majoritarian Dilemma:* An unelected bench of judges wields an absolute veto over constitutional amendments enacted by a two-thirds majority of elected representatives, challenging the core premise of representative democracy.
- *Metaphysical Drift over Textualism:* Critics contend that the doctrine gives primacy to judicial "metaphysics" over the explicit, black-and-white text of Article 368. By creating non-textual, implied limitations on constituent power, the Court elevated natural law and judges' personal ideologies over positive constitutional law.
- *The "Chancellor's Foot" (Subjectivity):* The Court's steadfast refusal to provide an exhaustive definition or list of basic features leaves the doctrine open-ended and unpredictable, fueling allegations of judicial overreach (*Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record v. UOI*, 2015 / NJAC Case).

**Conclusion:**
Despite its academic and counter-majoritarian infirmities, the Basic Structure doctrine serves as an indispensable institutional safety valve. By preserving the "Golden Triangle" (Articles 14, 19, 21), federalism, secularism, and judicial review, it ensures that constitutional morality prevails over transitory legislative majorities.

### Question 2 (UPSC PSIR 2015 / 2021 Archetype — 15 Marks / 150 Words)
**"Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles of State Policy are not antithetical, but complementary components of an indivisible social charter." Discuss in the light of Granville Austin's observations and judicial pronouncements.**

#### Model Answer:

The perceived conflict between Part III (Fundamental Rights) and Part IV (Directive Principles) stemmed from a historical misunderstanding of the Constituent Assembly's architecture. While Part III embodies classical liberal civil-political rights (negative injunctions), Part IV incorporates socialist-egalitarian goals (positive obligations).

Granville Austin (*The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation*) conceptualized the Indian Constitution as a "Seamless Web" aimed at a socio-economic revolution. Austin termed Parts III and IV the "conscience of the Constitution," emphasizing that they form an indivisible scheme rather than competing hierarchies.

This organic complementarity is grounded in pre-independence constitutional thought:
1. *The Motilal Nehru Report (1928)* integrated both categories of rights into a single unified list.
2. *Sir B.N. Rau and Dr. B.R. Ambedkar* bifurcated them solely due to post-independence material scarcity, substituting political accountability (elections) for judicial enforceability under Article 37 to prevent an immediate fiscal-constitutional crisis.

Judicially, while *Champakam Dorairajan* (1951) and *Golaknath* (1967) artificially subordinated or elevated these parts, *Minerva Mills* (1980) permanently restored equilibrium. CJI Y.V. Chandrachud ruled that Parts III and IV are *"two wheels of a chariot"* and that the harmony between them is itself a basic feature of the Constitution.

This convergence culminated post-*Maneka Gandhi* (1978), where the Supreme Court read socio-economic directives (clean environment, legal aid, livelihood) directly into Article 21, and Parliament formally elevated Article 45 into Article 21A (Right to Education via the 86th Amendment), proving their essential indivisibility.
