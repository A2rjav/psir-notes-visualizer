### I. The Concept and Philosophy of Grassroots Democracy
1. **Core Conceptual Definition:**
   - **Grassroots Democracy** refers to political processes and institutional designs driven by the lowest civic tiers of society, operating on the principle of **subsidiarity**—the idea that central authority should perform only those tasks which cannot be performed effectively at a more immediate or local level.
   - It marks a transition from **representative (indirect) democracy** to **participatory and deliberative democracy**.
   - As emphasized in lecture: *"Grassroot Democracy is the essence of Democracy. It is an empowerment of people at the grassroot level. Grassroot democracy aims at empowering the powerless & giving voice to voiceless."*

2. **The Essence of Empowerment:**
   - In standard elite or Schumpeterian models of democracy, citizens are passive electors who periodically exercise their franchise to select competing ruling elites.
   - Grassroots democracy subverts this hierarchy by treating citizens as active co-producers of governance, development policy, and social justice.

### II. Indian Philosophical Traditions of Decentralization

```
                          INDIAN VISIONS OF GRASSROOTS DEMOCRACY
                                             │
            ┌────────────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────┐
            ▼                                ▼                                ▼
    MAHATMA GANDHI                       M.N. ROY                     DEENDAYAL UPADHYAY
      [Gram Swaraj]                [Radical Democracy]                    [Antyodaya]
• Oceanic circles of power         • Organized, party-less polity    • Uplift of the last person
• Village republics                • People's Committees             • Decentralized indigenous
• Stateless, enlightened anarchy   • Rational, human-centric           economy & Integral Humanism
```

#### 1. Mahatma Gandhi: *Gram Swaraj* (Village Republics)
- **Critique of Western Parliamentary Democracy:** In *Hind Swaraj* (1909), Gandhi famously criticized the British Parliament as a "sterile woman" (it cannot perform good work without outside pressure) and a "prostitute" (it changes masters under party whips). He argued that top-down parliamentary democracy centralizes violence and coercive state power.
- **Oceanic Circles vs. Pyramidal Power:** Gandhi rejected the hierarchical pyramid where millions at the bottom support a tiny elite at the apex. Instead, he envisioned an **"oceanic circle"** where the individual is at the center, ready to perish for the village, the village for the cluster of villages, till at last the whole becomes one life composed of individuals, never aggressive in their arrogance but ever humble.
- **Enlightened Anarchy:** The village is an autonomous, self-sufficient economic and political unit (*Gram Swaraj*), governed by a panchayat of five elders chosen annually by all adult villagers.

#### 2. M. N. Roy: *Radical Democracy* (New Humanism)
- **Critique of Formal Representative Democracy:** M.N. Roy argued that parliamentary democracy delegitimizes the individual, reducing sovereignty to an occasional vote cast under the hypnotic demagogy of organized political parties.
- **Party-less, Organized Democracy:** In his *Twenty-Two Theses* (1946), Roy advocated for **Radical Democracy**, where political parties are dismantled, and political authority vests directly in a network of **People's Committees** (*Lok Samitis*) at the grassroots.
- **Direct Citizen Control:** The People's Committees would nominate candidates, recall representatives, conduct referendums, and wield executive-legislative authority locally, making democracy real and deliberative.

#### 3. Pt. Deendayal Upadhyay: *Antyodaya* & Integral Humanism (*Ekatma Manavavada*)
- **Integral Humanism:** Upadhyay rejected both Western capitalist materialism and Marxist dialectical materialism, proposing an indigenous philosophy catering to the four-fold needs of body (*Kama*), mind (*Artha*), intellect (*Dharma*), and soul (*Moksha*).
- **Antyodaya (Upliftment of the Last Person):** Economic and political progress should not be measured by the wealth of the top tier (GDP/GNP) but by the wellbeing of the poorest person at the periphery (*Antyodaya*).
- **Decentralized Polity & Swadeshi:** True democracy requires administrative and economic decentralization, where local communities control production, resource allocation, and governance based on human scale and ecological balance.

### III. Justifications: Good Governance, Sustainable Development & Political Education

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                        THREE PILLARS OF GRASSROOTS DEMOCRACY                           │
├─────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────┤
│    1. GOOD GOVERNANCE   │  2. SUSTAINABLE DEVELOPMENT  │ 3. POLITICAL EDUCATION        │
├─────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────┤
│ • Community oversight   │ • Agenda 21 (Rio Summit 1992)│ • School of citizenship       │
│ • Downward accountability│ • Chapter 28: Local Agenda 21│ • Capacity building           │
│ • Transparency & Social │ • Common Pool Resources      │ • Civic virtue & Tocqueville's│
│   Audit (Gram Sabha)    │   (Elinor Ostrom thesis)     │   "spirit of liberty"         │
└─────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────┘
```

#### 1. Good Governance (Accountability and Transparency)
- The World Bank (1992 report *Governance and Development*) defined Good Governance across eight attributes: Participatory, Consensus-oriented, Accountable, Transparent, Responsive, Effective and Efficient, Equitable and Inclusive, and Rule of Law.
- Bureaucratic centralism breeds asymmetric information, rent-seeking, and elite leakage. Grassroots governance enables **downward accountability** through institutions like the **Gram Sabha**, social audits, and citizen charters.

#### 2. Sustainable Development (Agenda 21, Rio Earth Summit 1992)
- Chapter 28 of **Agenda 21** explicitly recognized that because so many problems and solutions being addressed by Agenda 21 have their roots in local activities, the participation and cooperation of **local authorities** is a determining factor in fulfilling objectives.
- Nobel laureate **Elinor Ostrom** (*Governing the Commons*, 1990) demonstrated empirically that localized community institutions manage Common Pool Resources (water bodies, grazing lands, local forests) far more sustainably than either centralized state bureaucracies or privatized markets.

#### 3. Political Education and Training (Tocqueville & Mill)
- **Alexis de Tocqueville (*Democracy in America*, 1835):**
  > *"Town meetings are to liberty what primary schools are to science; they bring it within the people's reach, they teach men how to use and how to enjoy it. A nation may establish a system of free government, but without municipal institutions it cannot have the spirit of liberty."*
- **John Stuart Mill (*Considerations on Representative Government*, 1861):**
  Local representative bodies act as political training grounds. Without practical engagement in local affairs, citizens cannot understand broader national policies and remain parochial subjects rather than self-governing citizens.

### IV. The Great Constituent Assembly Debate: Gandhians vs. Modernists

```
                          CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY CLASH (1946-1949)
                                             │
                     ┌───────────────────────┴───────────────────────┐
                     ▼                                               ▼
             GANDHIAN SCHOOL                                 MODERNIST SCHOOL
     (Shriman Narayan, H.V. Kamath)                  (Ambedkar & Jawaharlal Nehru)
• Decentralized village polity                  • Centralized modern state
• Villages as basic unit of Constitution         • Village as "sink of localism & communalism"
• Minimum state, moral self-rule                 • State-led industrialization & social engineering
```

1. **The Gandhian Perspective:**
   - Championed by members like Shriman Narayan Agarwal (*The Gandhian Constitution for Free India*, 1946), H.V. Kamath, and Shibban Lal Saksena.
   - Demanded that the Constitution be built from the village upward, with direct elections at the village level and indirect elections for district, provincial, and national legislatures.

2. **The Modernist Critique (Ambedkar's Fierce Rebuke):**
   - Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, addressing the Constituent Assembly on November 4, 1948, delivered his historic critique of Indian villages:
     > *"That they have survived through all vicissitudes may be a fact. But mere survival has no value. The question is on what plane they have survived... What is the village but a sink of localism, a den of ignorance, narrow-mindedness and communalism? I am glad that the Draft Constitution has discarded the village and adopted the individual as its unit."*
   - For Dalits and oppressed castes, the traditional village panchayat was an instrument of feudal Brahmanical hegemony, caste atrocities, and untouchability. Modern state institutions, central law, and universal individual citizenship provided the only shield against village tyranny.

3. **Nehru's Vision:**
   - Nehru favored modern scientific planning, heavy industrialization, and a centralized developmental state to unify a newly partitioned, diverse nation. Villages were seen as economically backward and incapable of driving rapid modernization.

4. **The Compromise:**
   - Gandhians lost the main institutional battle. Panchayats were relegated to **Article 40** in **Part IV (Directive Principles of State Policy)**:
     > *"The State shall take steps to organise village panchayats and endow them with such powers and authority as may be necessary to enable them to function as units of self-government."*
   - Being in Part IV, Article 40 was non-justiciable, leaving Panchayati Raj to the discretionary mercy of individual state legislatures.

5. **The Glaring Neglect of Urban Local Bodies:**
   - While rural Panchayats received mention in Article 40, **Municipalities and Urban Administration were completely omitted from the Directive Principles**.
   - Urban local government was relegated solely to **Entry 5 of the State List (List II, Seventh Schedule)**: *"Local government, that is to say, the constitution and powers of municipal corporations, improvement trusts, district boards, mining settlement authorities and other local authorities for the purpose of local self-government or village administration."*

### V. Ambiguity in the Institutional Design of Panchayats

```
                     CONSTITUTIONAL & FUNCTIONAL AMBIGUITY
                                       │
            ┌──────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────┐
            ▼                                                     ▼
   INSTITUTIONS OF SELF-GOVERNMENT                        DEVELOPMENT AGENCIES
      (Art. 40 & Art. 243G)                         (Actual Practice & State Laws)
• Sovereign local legislative space              • Mere delivery conduits for schemes
• Autonomous taxing & policing powers             • Dependent on state/central funds
• Subsidiarity & democratic voice                 • Restricted to economic planning
```

1. **The Semantic & Legal Conflict:**
   - Article 40 and Article 243G describe Panchayats as **"institutions of self-government"** (*swashasan*).
   - Yet, in statutory execution, the 73rd Amendment assigns two primary functions:
     * *(a)* The preparation of plans for economic development and social justice.
     * *(b)* The implementation of schemes for economic development and social justice as may be entrusted to them (including those in relation to the 29 matters listed in the Eleventh Schedule).

2. **Deprivation of Sovereign & Regulatory Powers:**
   - Panchayats were **never granted police powers, maintenance of public order, or judicial/regulatory authority**.
   - They were designed not as a genuine "third tier of federal government" with legislative autonomy, but as **executive delivery arms** for poverty alleviation and central/state welfare schemes (e.g., MGNREGA, PMGSY, PMAY).

#### Detailed Breakdown of Phase I (1950s–1960s: Enthusiasm)
- **Community Development Programme (CDP - October 2, 1952):**
  - Conceived with American Ford Foundation assistance, CDP divided the country into Development Blocks.
  - Introduced the philosophy of **"Development Administration"**—civil servants (BDOs, VLWs) were tasked to be the vanguard of India's peaceful social transformation.
  - The **Block Development Officer (BDO)** was idealized as the *"friend, philosopher, and guide"* of the villagers.
- **Why CDP Failed:**
  - Bureaucracy possessed a rigid, colonial, command-and-control mindset; it could not generate participatory popular enthusiasm.
  - People treated the schemes as government charity (*sarkari daan*) rather than community action.
  - Outcome: CDP created physical administrative infrastructure (block offices, primary schools, roads) but utterly failed to mobilize democratic participation.
- **Balwant Rai Mehta Committee (1957):**
  - Appointed by the Committee on Plan Projects (Planning Commission) to assess CDP and NES.
  - Concluded: Rural development cannot succeed without community participation; community participation requires institutional empowerment via representative elected bodies.
  - Recommended the establishment of **"Democratic Decentralisation"** through a **3-tier Panchayati Raj system**:
    1. **Gram Panchayat** at the village level (directly elected).
    2. **Panchayat Samiti** at the block level (indirectly elected, the main executive body).
    3. **Zilla Parishad** at the district level (advisory and coordinating body, chaired by District Collector).
- **The Golden Dawn at Nagaur:**
  - Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru shedding his initial reservations, inaugurated Panchayati Raj at **Nagaur, Rajasthan on October 2, 1959** (Gandhi Jayanti). Andhra Pradesh followed next. By the early 1960s, almost all states enacted PRI legislation.

#### Detailed Breakdown of Phase II (1970s–1980s: Stagnation & Decline)
- **Why Stagnation? The Dual Dialectic:**
  1. **Insecurity of State-Level Political Leadership:**
     - Decentralization created alternative power centers. Young, energetic leaders emerged at Panchayat and Block levels, challenging entrenched MLAs, MPs, and traditional dominant caste elites.
     - In an over-centralized Indian federalism where States themselves felt reduced to **"Glorified Municipalities"** vis-à-vis the Union Government, Chief Ministers were unwilling to share their already truncated powers with PRIs.
  2. **Bureaucratic Hostility & Sabotage:**
     - Bureaucracy viewed PRIs as rivals usurping their executive patronage, discretionary spending, and authority.
     - Bureaucratic non-cooperation, refusal to work under elected representatives, and regular supersession/suspension of Panchayats by District Collectors became institutional norms.
  3. **Union-Driven Centralization:**
     - In the 1970s and 1980s, the Union Government bypassed local institutions entirely by floating centralized, vertical target-oriented schemes (IRDP, DPAP, CADP) implemented exclusively through line departments and DRDAs (District Rural Development Agencies).

- **Key Committees of the Stagnation Era:**
  - **Ashok Mehta Committee (1977 - Janata Party):** Recommended replacement of 3-tier system with a **2-tier system** (Zilla Parishad at district level + Mandal Panchayat covering 15,000–20,000 population); open participation of political parties; constitutional protection to prevent arbitrary supersession.
  - **G.V.K. Rao Committee (1985 - Planning Commission):** Concluded that development had become bureaucratized and alienated from the people, characterizing PRIs as **"grass without roots"**. Recommended making Zilla Parishad the pivotal body for all district-level development planning.
  - **L.M. Singhvi Committee (1986 - Rajiv Gandhi Govt):** Recommended that Panchayati Raj Institutions must be **constitutionally recognized, protected, and preserved** through a new constitutional chapter. Also advocated for Nyaya Panchayats and financial autonomy.

#### Phase III (1990s: Revival — 73rd & 74th Amendments, 1992)
- Enacted during the P.V. Narasimha Rao government, adding **Part IX** (Articles 243 to 243O) and **Eleventh Schedule** (29 functional items) for Panchayats, and **Part IXA** (Articles 243P to 243ZG) and **Twelfth Schedule** (18 items) for Municipalities.
- Established constitutional floor: mandatory 3-tier structure (states < 20 lakh exempt from intermediate tier); 5-year fixed tenure with mandatory elections within 6 months of dissolution; independent **State Election Commission (SEC)**; **State Finance Commission (SFC)** every 5 years; **one-third (33%) reservation for women** in seats and chairpersons; proportional reservation for SCs and STs; institutionalization of **Gram Sabha**.

#### Phase IV (21st Century: Disillusionment)
- **Mani Shankar Aiyar Committee Report (2012):**
  - Appointed by the Ministry of Panchayati Raj to evaluate PRI performance after two decades of the 73rd Amendment.
  - Formulated the damning critique: the primary consequence of constitutional devolution has been the **"decentralisation of corruption"** rather than democratic empowerment.
  - Warned that India has installed a **"Bad Panchayati Raj, which is worse than no Panchayati Raj"**, characterized by bureaucratic hegemony, fiscal starvation, lack of administrative autonomy, and elite capture.

### Model Answer 1: Evolution and Causes for Weaknesses of Panchayats

#### **Question:**
> *Discuss the Evolution of Panchayati Raj in India and analyze the primary causes responsible for the continuing weaknesses of Panchayats.*  
> *(20 Marks / 250 Words — UPSC CSE PSIR Paper 1)*

#### **Structure of the Answer:**
1. **Introduction:**
   - Define Grassroots Democracy as the institutionalization of the subsidiarity principle and participatory democracy.
   - Mention the constitutional trajectory from Article 40 (DPSP) to the landmark 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act (1992).

2. **Evolutionary Trajectory (The Four Phases):**
   - **Phase I (1950s–1960s) — Enthusiasm:** Failure of top-down Community Development Programme (1952) led to the Balwant Rai Mehta Committee (1957) recommending democratic decentralisation via a 3-tier structure, inaugurated at Nagaur (1959).
   - **Phase II (1970s–1980s) — Stagnation & Decline:** Political insecurity among state elites and bureaucratic hostility sidelined PRIs into "grass without roots" (G.V.K. Rao Committee, 1985; Ashok Mehta Committee, 1977).
   - **Phase III (1990s) — Revival:** L.M. Singhvi Committee (1986) recommendations culminated in the 73rd Amendment Act, providing uniform constitutional status, reservation for women, SEC, and SFC.
   - **Phase IV (21st Century) — Disillusionment:** As observed by the Mani Shankar Aiyar Committee (2012), devolution resulted in "decentralisation of corruption" rather than genuine democratic empowerment.

3. **Primary Causes for Weaknesses of Panchayats:**
   - **The Crisis of the 3 Fs (Funds, Functions, Functionaries):**
     * *Funds:* Extreme fiscal dependency; internal revenue generation of PRIs is under 5% of their total expenditure. State Finance Commissions' recommendations are routinely ignored by state governments.
     * *Functions:* Ambiguity between mandatory and discretionary provisions under Article 243G; lack of functional activity mapping.
     * *Functionaries:* Absence of dedicated bureaucratic cadres accountable to elected Panchayats; line departments (PWD, Health, Irrigation) bypass PRIs entirely.
   - **Bureaucratic Resistance & Patronage:** Bureaucrats treat Panchayats as subordinate implementation agencies rather than institutions of self-government, resisting the transfer of executive control.
   - **Political Insecurity of State Legislators:** MLAs and MPs perceive PRI chairpersons as emerging electoral rivals, actively curbing the devolution of real local authority.
   - **Elite Capture & Social Fault Lines:** Entrenched rural dominant castes capture resources; pervasive phenomenon of *Sarpanch Pati* undermines the substantive potential of women's reservation.

4. **Conclusion:**
   - Quote Alexis de Tocqueville: without autonomous local institutions, a nation cannot achieve the "spirit of liberty."
   - Conclude that overcoming these structural infirmities requires activity mapping, fiscal federalism at the third tier, strengthening Gram Sabha oversight, and transforming PRIs from development agencies into true institutions of self-government.

### Model Answer 2: 25-Year Critical Assessment of the 73rd Constitutional Amendment

#### **Question:**
> *Give an assessment of the working of Panchayats in India over the last 25 years. Has the 73rd Constitutional Amendment succeeded in deepening democracy at the grassroots?*  
> *(20 Marks / 250 Words — UPSC CSE PSIR Paper 1)*

#### **Structure of the Answer:**
1. **Introduction:**
   - Situate the 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act (1992) as a watershed institutional reform that transformed India into a three-tier federal polity, adding Part IX and the Eleventh Schedule (29 subjects).

2. **Major Achievements (Deepening of Democratic Substantive Space):**
   - **Democratic Broadbasing & Silent Revolution:** Created the world's largest democratic grassroots architecture with over 3 million elected representatives across ~2.5 lakh Gram Panchayats.
   - **Gender Empowerment & Agency:** The mandatory 33% reservation (expanded to 50% by over 20 states including Bihar, Kerala, Madhya Pradesh) brought over 1.4 million women into formal decision-making, gradually eroding patriarchal domination in local public spheres.
   - **Inclusion of Marginalized Communities:** Proportional reservation for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes dismantled historical monopolies of dominant feudal castes, facilitating symbolic and substantive political assertion.
   - **Institutionalization of Direct Deliberation:** The Gram Sabha provided a constitutional platform for participatory budgeting, beneficiary identification, and social audits (e.g., under MGNREGA).

3. **Critical Shortcomings & Pathologies (The Disillusionment):**
   - **"Decentralisation of Corruption":** As the Mani Shankar Aiyar Committee (2012) observed, corruption has traveled downstream without concomitant accountability, fostering a culture of rent-seeking at the panchayat level.
   - **Panchayats as Mere Implementation Conduits:** Despite Article 243G declaring them "institutions of self-government", state governments treat them as subservient execution agencies for tied, top-down central/state schemes.
   - **Fiscal Castration:** Panchayats remain fiscally crippled, unable to levy property or professional taxes effectively, relying almost wholly on central Finance Commission grants and tied state transfers.
   - **The *Sarpanch Pati* Phenomenon:** Patriarchal subversion where elected women representatives serve as figureheads while husbands or male relatives exercise real executive authority.
   - **Weakening of Parallel Bodies:** Creation of parallel bodies (e.g., user groups, DRDAs, water and forest committees) directly funded by line ministries further undermines PRI authority.

4. **Way Forward & Conclusion:**
   - Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2nd ARC) recommendations: Clear activity mapping based on the principle of subsidiarity; mandatory adoption of SFC recommendations; creating an independent rural municipal civil service.
   - Deepening grassroots democracy requires transitioning from formal electoral decentralization to substantive empowerment—ensuring that Panchayats govern as self-reliant, transparent, and accountable institutions of rural self-rule.
